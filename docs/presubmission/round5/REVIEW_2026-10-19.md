# TMLR Review: Right Order, Wrong Size

Oct 19, 2026

## Bottom line

The paper is squarely in TMLR's scope and unusually careful, but as written I'd expect reviewers to require substantial revision. Rejection with an invitation to resubmit is a realistic outcome.

It is one of the most careful reproduction studies I've read. TMLR explicitly invites reproducibility studies of previously published claims, and accepts papers that meet its criteria even when the contribution is modest ([editorial policies](https://www.jmlr.org/tmlr/editorial-policies.html)). Its guidance also says a reproducibility report that probes a published method's robustness and gives actionable lessons can satisfy the interest criterion; your §9 is exactly that ([acceptance criteria](https://www.jmlr.org/tmlr/acceptance-criteria.html)).

Two things drive the expected outcome:

- **Presentation, which at TMLR is not cosmetic.** The most important criterion, whether claims rest on convincing and clear evidence, explicitly includes the clarity of the narrative.
- **Five or six technical points** that anyone in model-based RL or uncertainty quantification would raise, quite possibly the original authors as reviewers.

Most fixes are writing and inference-only analysis; two items need a day or two of CPU.

## What's strong

- **Gradient-level equivalence with the released module before training (App. A).** Few reproductions earn the right to attribute later discrepancies to the method rather than their own wiring.
- **Statistical discipline most ML papers lack.** You keep arenas separate, count independent trajectories, use dimension-preserving permutation nulls, and withdrew your own binomial P-values when they turned out wrong.
- **§6.3's σ = 0 derivation plus the M-50 synthetic test.** It is crisp, transferable, and the cleanest result in the paper.
- **The free baselines for the trust metric.** The step-size result is uncomfortable, and that is exactly the kind of finding reviewers respect.
- **Two concrete pipeline defects with line-level fixes:** the stale action and the unmarked episode boundaries.
- **The pre-registration record and the retraction you kept.** TMLR itself was presented at the NeurIPS 2021 Pre-Registration Workshop ([TMLR home](https://jmlr.csail.mit.edu/tmlr/index.html)), so the venue will value it. See issue 1 on how much space it gets.

## Major issues

### 1. Clarity and length

TMLR has no page limit, but expects length to be justified by content and warns that unusually long main bodies delay review ([author guidelines](https://jmlr.org/tmlr/author-guide.html)). Above 12 pages of main content, the normal review timeline isn't enforced ([FAQ](https://jmlr.csail.mit.edu/tmlr/faq.html)). Your main text runs to about 26 pages and reads more like an audit log than a paper:

- The \~320-word abstract carries nearly twenty numbers and capitalised verdicts. Phrases like "no reading favouring it" can't be parsed by a newcomer.
- The body leans on internal identifiers (M-23, X3, R-39, S-15, C-09) and labels like "SURVIVES entry-res ONLY".
- Results recur verbatim with their hedges. "+0.2816 \[+0.1978, +0.3896\]" appears about ten times, and "17 of 36" more often.
- Several appendices are revision history ("the passages below stood there as follows").
- Figure 1 shows pre-registration lead times. A reader's first figure should be a finding.
- Process detail belongs in the supplement: queue logs, CPU-overlap counts, the history rewrite, and a clean-clone gate "published as failing".

**Fix:**

- Target 12–15 pages.
- Write a \~200-word abstract with at most five numbers.
- State each result once with its scope (arena, n, checkpoint), and cross-reference it after that.
- Use plain-language verdicts in the body; keep rule IDs and committed labels in App. E.
- Use two significant figures in the body.
- Make the appendices standalone sections.
- Replace Figure 1 with a headline result.

### 2. You reproduce v1 preprints, but both papers now have versions of record

RWM appeared at [NeurIPS 2025](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/123991) and the follow-up at [ICLR 2026](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/10018013). Reference 2 also pairs v1 with a later title: v1 was titled "Offline Robotic World Model: Learning Robotic Policies without a Physics Simulator" ([arXiv v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16680v1)).

Reviewers will ask which tested claims survive in the published versions. One matters directly: the current abstract says uncertainty is "effectively propagated over long horizons" ([arXiv, current version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16680)).

Your §6.4 finding, that the rollout feeds the ensemble mean through a single hidden state, bears on exactly that claim. If v3 changed the rollout instead, your pinned code isn't the method of record. Either way, add a claim crosswalk from v1 to the versions of record, and test against the published text wherever they differ.

Reading v1 ([PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.16680v3)) turned up three corrections, all in your favour or easy to fix:

- **§6.1 understates what the follow-up claimed.** You say it observes only that the aleatoric term stays low. But v1 §4.1 asserts that the learned variance "captures aleatoric uncertainty effectively", a direct claim that §6.3 refutes. Quote it.
- **The shared trunk is documented in the original.** v1 §4.1 places the ensemble heads after a shared recurrent feature extractor and calls them bootstrap ensembles. §6.4 ("not five models") currently reads like a discovery; credit the design to the paper and make the measured consequence the contribution. Also, your App. R says the heads differ only in initialisation; if the code does no per-head resampling, "bootstrap" is a paper-code gap that belongs in App. H.
- **C-09 may not be a gap.** The v1 world-model training table sets the forecast decay to 1.0, so App. H's "the paper describes a decay" doesn't hold for RWM-O. Check RWM's own table before claiming it.

### 3. The headline calibration numbers mix three different gaps

"33.4× too small at h = 100" combines:

- **One-step versus accumulated error.** The ensemble mean is what the model feeds forward as the next observation, so disagreement at step t is a one-step spread at a shared state. Whenever errors compound, its ratio to accumulated error will grow with depth, even if it were perfectly calibrated for one-step error. MOPO's penalty is meant to bound one-step model error at visited states (Yu et al., 2020), so "no single λ fits" and "deep-rollout states are under-penalised" don't follow from the horizon curve.
- **Epistemic versus total uncertainty.** In a near-deterministic simulator, one-step error is mostly model inadequacy, which ensemble spread doesn't estimate. You cite Abbas et al. on this, but the headline ignores it.
- **Residual one-step miscalibration on held-out data.** This is the number that matters for the penalty's actual job, and your data already contain it. At h = 1 out of sample, the ensemble-5 arms have ρ = 2.1× and 36% ±1σ coverage; the independent ensemble has 1.4× and 50%. That is a moderate shortfall of the kind deep ensembles commonly show, an order of magnitude from 33.4×.

Testing the original's trend claim against accumulated error is legitimate: v1's §5.1 compares epistemic uncertainty with the accumulated prediction error over the rollout. The problem is reading the resulting ratio as a calibration failure of the penalty.

Two related points:

- **"In-sample measurement biases toward better calibration" (§6.2) isn't safe for ensemble disagreement.** Heads fitted to the same targets agree most where they were trained. The released checkpoint in-sample at h = 1 (8.3×) is about four times worse than your held-out arms (2.1×). Your ensemble-5 arms let you check this directly: 16 in-sample versus 4 held-out trajectories.
- **State the evidence that the checkpoint trained on exactly these rows.** §7.5 and App. F say the release can't reproduce the checkpoint, and the first author describes a different training setup. If the CSV is a separate recording, every released-checkpoint number flips from in-sample to held-out.

**Fix:** restructure §6 around the three gaps and lead with the third. Then run one inference-only experiment: roll the released checkpoint out PETS-style (TS∞: each head feeds back its own prediction through its own hidden state; Chua et al., 2018), and score the propagated spread against accumulated error.

That directly tests the "propagated over long horizons" claim. If it closes much of the gap, it becomes your most actionable result.

### 4. Held-out inference at n = 4 is weaker than its intervals suggest

With four clusters the percentile bootstrap is unreliable (Cameron, Gelbach & Miller, 2008):

- Its interval sits inside \[min, max\] of the four values.
- &#91;min, max\] covers a continuous distribution's median only 87.5% of the time.
- An exact sign-flip test on four paired differences can't go below two-sided p = 0.125.

So at n = 4, "the interval excludes zero" (and "survives Holm") essentially means "all four agree in sign". Several verdicts are weaker than their labels: §5's by-horizon gaps, §5.2, §5.3, and M-44/M-68/M-49.

**Fix:**

- Lead §5 with X3 (20 trajectories, 10 of 10 episodes, p = 0.002), and demote the 10k held-out factor to supporting evidence.
- Report exact randomisation p-values beside every n = 4 bootstrap.
- Reuse X3's folds for the held-out calibration experiments (M-43, the Arm A recalibration, M-44). By your App. B timings that is roughly 25 CPU-hours, or about 50 if M-68 and M-49 come along. It moves your "supported but not established" and "under-powered" claims from n = 4 to n = 20.

### 5. §5.2 and §5.3 spend a lot of space on weak tests

- **§5.2 tests an unconverged budget.** It runs at 2,500 iterations with every run still improving, on 0.133% of the data, and its verdict reverses with longer training. The original's claim is also a trade-off with training time, which you don't test. Either train to a validation plateau or cut it to a paragraph ("at small budgets, shorter histories learn faster").
- **§5.3's baselines are untuned.** They run at RWM's learning rate and budget. Three rows diverge, the RSSM is uninformative by your own account, and the size-matched transformer can't be told apart from RWM. "REPRODUCES" over-reads that; a three-point learning-rate sweep per baseline would make it a real test, otherwise call it consistent but weak.
- **Some labels read as negative findings.** "DOES NOT GENERALISE" for the under-powered M-43 is the main example. In the body, say inconclusive.

## Technical corrections

**a. M-65 reads the tails backwards.** Suppose you match mean|error|/σ to a Gaussian's 0.798. A heavy-tailed (or scale-mixture) error-to-σ ratio then over-covers at ±1σ and under-covers at ±2σ, so your 33 of 48 over-covering cells are that signature, not evidence against heavy tails.

Your own multipliers show the same thing. At h = 1 the mean-matching constant is 10.4 (§6.2) but the coverage-matching one is 6.3–6.8 (App. Y); for a Gaussian these coincide, and at h = 368 they nearly do (43.2 against 40.3–43.0).

Before recommending per-horizon rescaling, report ±2σ coverage under it, and expect shortfalls at short horizons.

**b. Figure 4's rate is mostly an Adam property.** Your configured learning rate, the released ANYmal-D setting used in every run, is the 1e-4 listed in the RWM-O training table ([v1, Table S8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.16680v3)), so −9.39e-5 per iteration is 0.94× the learning rate.

- Adam moves a parameter whose gradient keeps one sign by roughly the learning rate per step, whatever the gradient's size.
- The plotted parameter (log Δ) gets a deterministic push from the bound term: 0.2, against 0.05 from the state loss (App. J).
- So the 6e-7 spread across runs is an optimiser property, and only the sign flip speaks to the objective.

Plot σ itself, not only the clamp width. And recast §7.5's "clock" as Adam's per-step bound (Kingma & Ba, 2015, §2.1), which is a cleaner argument than a rate fitted on 22 runs.

**c. "Too small by" has two meanings.** ρ = 33.4 against a calibrated 0.80 means mean-matching needs ×42, while restoring 68% coverage needs ×29–32 (App. Y). Pick one meaning and use it throughout.

**d. nRMSE's floor isn't 1.0.** With the mean per-dimension σ in the denominator, the training-mean predictor scores RMS(σ)/mean(σ), which is at least 1. Either normalise by the RMS of the per-dimension σ or report the mean predictor directly.

Then add it, and a seasonal-naive floor (repeat the last gait cycle, the standard floor for periodic series), to the long-horizon tables. In App. L at h = 368, the released checkpoint scores 0.905 and every architecture baseline is above 1 (MLP-AR 1.44, transformer-AR 2.01). If 1.0 is a constant predictor, every baseline loses to a constant, which changes how §5.3 reads.

**e. Ranking claims should use rank correlation.** §3.1 uses Pearson "because a ranking claim is about order". That rationale points to Spearman or Kendall, which is why Lu et al. report Spearman.

**f. Smaller fixes in §6.3:**

- The floor doesn't "freeze". App. J gives the state loss a 0.0703 gradient on `min_logstd`, and App. H says it drifts 5.2× slower than log Δ; say instead that it gets no gradient from the bound term.
- "Provably drives σ to zero" should read "the objective's optimum is σ = 0". The derivation is per step, and in autoregressive training the fed-back sample adds another pathway. The teacher-forced arm's identical rate suggests that pathway doesn't matter; say so.
- The PETS survey count is circular as worded, because "examined" is defined as "carries the construction". Say: 13 repositories inspected, 10 implement the bounded head, 1 optionally trains it on sampled squared error.

## Missing related work

- **Multi-step model training:** Venkatraman et al. (2015, DaD), Bengio et al. (2015, scheduled sampling), Talvitie (2017), Lambert et al. (2021). Autoregressive training beating teacher forcing at long horizons, and losing at one step, is well established; saying so sets the prior for §5.
- **Conformal prediction:** §6.7 is split conformal with a σ-normalised score per horizon. Relevant work: Angelopoulos & Bates; Stankevičiūtė et al. (NeurIPS 2021, per-horizon conformal intervals for multi-step forecasts); Lindemann et al. (RA-L 2023, robotics). The framing gives finite-sample coverage guarantees and tells you how many held-out episodes the recipe needs.
- **Shared-torso RL ensembles:** Osband et al. 2016 (Bootstrapped DQN) and 2018 (randomised priors) are the canonical RL precedent for §6.4 and its fix.
- **RL evaluation statistics:** Agarwal et al. 2021; Henderson et al. 2018.
- **Pre-registration in ML:** the NeurIPS 2020–21 pre-registration workshops.

## Smaller points

- **Title.** "Verified" can read as verifying the method's results, whereas you verify the code. Consider naming the scope (the dynamics model).
- **"First we are aware of" claims.** Drop them; any reproduction could make them.
- **Table reference.** App. H cites Table S9 for the world model's training length. In v1 that is the MOPO-PPO table; the world model's is Table S8 (both say 2,500).
- **Upstream report.** Say whether you reported the action misalignment upstream, and what came back.
- **Clean-clone gate.** Explain the three values that fail it, or drop the gate from the paper.
- **Scoring rule.** Add a proper scoring rule (CRPS or Gaussian NLL) beside coverage, so the sharpness cost of per-horizon rescaling is visible.
- **Tone.** Some headings read as adversarial, such as "The structural excuse does not survive" and "evaluation is the broken one". Neutral wording costs nothing with reviewers who may be the original authors.
- **Conflict of interest.** The original first author knows your identity through the correspondence. TMLR asks authors to report conflicts not covered by their institutional history, so declare it ([author guidelines](https://jmlr.csail.mit.edu/tmlr/author-guide.html)).
- **MLRC timing.** If NeurIPS's reproducibility track was a goal, the 2026 cycle required TMLR submission by September 30, 2026 and acceptance as is or with minor revisions ([MLRC 2026](https://blog.neurips.cc/?p=1530)). Either way, a tight version is what would earn TMLR's Reproducibility Certification.
