Abstract
We reimplement the dynamics model of the Robotic World Model (RWM) and test the
uncertainty its follow-up, RWM-U, uses as a reward penalty in offline policy optimisation.
Our rebuild matches the release’s gradients. RWM’s claim that training on its own rollouts,
not one-step targets, lowers long-horizon error holds on held-out data: holding each of ten
episodes out once, at the original’s training length, it wins on every one at the pre-registered
horizon; on short windows at one step it loses. RWM-U’s ensemble disagreement ranks errors
right but sizes them wrong: it ranks them better than the forecast step (Spearman +0.90
at a hundred steps), yet in every ensemble measured, error exceeds what the spread implies
even at one step. The release feeds every member the ensemble mean, so spread does not
track accumulated error: at 100 steps on the released checkpoint one-sigma coverage is
4.6% against 68%, and rolling each member on its own predictions raises it to 33%. Each
member’s variance collapses: the implemented loss, squared error on a sampled prediction,
is minimised at zero. Rescaling the released checkpoint’s spread per horizon (fitted on nine
episodes, scored on the tenth) restores one-sigma coverage, not two-sigma. We fix two
pipeline defects. One robot, gait and dataset; no policy is trained.
1 Introduction
Offline model-based reinforcement learning trains a robot’s policy inside a learned simulator. The Robotic
World Model (RWM; Li, Krause and Hutter) learns such a simulator for a quadruped from recorded robot
data, and its follow-up, RWM-U, adds an ensemble of predictors whose disagreement penalises the reward,
in the manner of MOPO (Yu et al., 2020). A policy can be trusted inside the simulator only as far as the
simulator’s errors are known, so the penalty has two jobs: to rank where the model is more likely to be
wrong, and to say by how much. We test whether it does either, on the released code and checkpoint and
on models we rebuilt from the papers’ descriptions.
What we found, in plain words.The rebuild matches the release’s gradients before any training, so
later differences belong to the method and not to our wiring (§3.1). RWM’s central claim, that training on
the model’s own rollouts lowers long-horizon error more than training on one-step targets (teacher forcing),
holds on episodes the models never saw: with each of ten episodes held out once, rollout training wins on
every episode at the pre-registered horizon, and on short windows at one step it loses (§4.1, Figure 1a). The
other two claims we tested get only weak tests (§4.2, §4.3).
RWM-U’s ensemble disagreement ranks errors correctly and sizes them wrongly, in three separate ways.
Even at one step, every ensemble’s error exceeds what its spread implies (§5.2). The five members share one
trunk, so they can disagree only about what that trunk carries (§5.3). And the release feeds every member
the ensemble mean, so the spread does not grow with the horizon as the error does; rolling each member
on its own predictions, which is not the released method, partly repairs this (§5.4, Figure 1b). Separately,
the per-member variance the method discards collapses because the implemented loss is minimised at zero
(§5.5). The spread ranks error better than the forecast step does (§5.6), and rescaling it per horizon restores

2 Background and related work
RWM and RWM-U.RWM (Li, Krause and Hutter) trains a recurrent dynamics model on a quadruped’s
proprioceptive data and uses it as a simulator for policy learning. RWM-U’s ensemble of five predictors
shares one state trunk, as its authors state, and penalises the reward by the members’ disagreement. The
penalty follows MOPO (Yu et al., 2020), which penalises the reward by an ensemble uncertainty estimate,
and MOReL (Kidambi et al., 2020), which detects unknown states by pairwise ensemble disagreement; both
branch short rollouts from real states, as MBPO does (Janner et al., 2019). As we read MOPO, the penalty
is meant to bound the model’s one-step error at the states the policy visits. That is the use against which
§5.2 measures the spread.
Multi-step training.Training a dynamics model on its own multi-step predictions rather than on one-step
targets is an established remedy for compounding error; Venkatraman et al. (2015), Bengio et al. (2015) for
sequences, Talvitie (2017) and Lambert et al. (2021) are examples. That sets the prior for §4: a long-horizon
advantage for rollout training is what these methods aim at, so the direction of RWM’s claim is expected,
and the test is whether it holds on held-out episodes of this dataset and what happens at one step.
Ensembles that share a trunk.Deep ensembles are trained from different initialisations and data or-
derings (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017). Reinforcement-learning ensembles with a shared torso include
Bootstrapped DQN (Osband et al., 2016) and randomised prior functions (Osband et al., 2018). That heads
sharing a trunk under-report disagreement is established (Lee et al. (2015), Fort, Hu and Lakshminarayanan
(2019), Wen et al. (2020) and Havasi et al. (2021)). RWM-U documents its shared trunk; §5.3 asks what
the sharing costs.
Calibration, recalibration and conformal prediction.Lu et al. (2022) compare uncertainty heuristics
for offline model-based reinforcement learning under a protocol built "to capture the specific covariate shift
induced by model-based RL", and report rank and bivariate correlation with true model error separately.
That is our distinction between ranking and scale, on the same family of penalties, and the closest precedent.
We add coverage measurements on a released checkpoint. Kuleshov et al. (2018) recalibrate regression
uncertainty, and Malik et al. (2019) do so for a dynamics model inside model-based reinforcement learning,
arguing that "good uncertainties must be calibrated". Our per-horizon rescaling (§5.7) fits a scale on some
held-out data and scores it on other held-out data, which resembles split conformal prediction (Angelopoulos
and Bates (2021), Stankeviči¯ ut˙ e et al. (2021), Lindemann et al. (2023)); we make no claim of the conformal
guarantee.
Evaluation statistics.Henderson et al. (2018) and Agarwal et al. (2021) are the reference points for
reporting reinforcement-learning results from few runs. Our intervals rest on few seeds and few trajectories,
so §3.4 reports the count beside every interval.
The PETS lineage.The bounded log-σhead is inherited from PETS (Chua et al., 2018); Appendix J asks
which public descendants inherit its objective as well. Seitzer et al. (2022) show failure modes ofσheads
trained by maximising likelihood; the released state loss is not a likelihood at all (§5.5).

We rebuilt the dynamics model from the papers’ descriptions and the released module: a recurrent (GRU)
ensemble whose members predict the next proprioceptive state with a mean head and a bounded log-σhead,
plus auxiliary heads for contact and termination. The paper describes two loss terms; the implementation has

3.3 Metrics and floors
Relative-L1is the reference’s own metric, which keeps our numbers comparable to the upstream’s printed
ones: at each forecast step, the summed absolute error over the state dimensions divided by the summed
absolute true state, on normalised states, averaged over trajectories and over the steps after the first 32,
which are teacher-forced. Its denominator can pass through zero, so we also reportnormalised RMSE
(nRMSE): the root of the squared error pooled over the state dimensions, divided by the dimensions’
mean standard deviation over the training episodes. A value of one is not the training-mean floor, because
that predictor scores the root-mean-square of the dimensions’ standard deviations over their mean, which
is at least one; we report three floors: predicting the training mean, holding the last state, and repeating a
seasonal pattern (§4.3; Appendix F defines them).
Coverage at±kσis the fraction of (trajectory, step, dimension) triples whose absolute error is within k
times the predictedσ; a calibrated Gaussian gives 68% at±1σand 95% at±2σ.The error-to-spread
ratioρis the mean absolute error over the mean predictedσ; a calibrated Gaussian hasρ= 0.80, so a
ratio of one is not calibration, and we reportρbeside that value."Too small by k"means one thing: the
factor k onσthat restores nominal±1σcoverage.Ranking statementsuse Spearman’s rank correlation,
because a ranking claim is about order and a rank statistic does not depend on scale or on a linear relation;
a rule committed with Pearson’s correlation keeps it, and we say so (Appendix L gives Kendall’s).
Curves are reported at horizons h = 1, 8, 32, 100, 128 and 368, each cumulative from the first forecast
step to h.h = 100is the method’s own imagination rollout length.h = 368is the upstream’s open-loop
diagnostic length, 400 minus the 32-step prefix, 3.7×longer than the method’s rollout and not a deployment
horizon.

3.4 Statistical protocol, pre-registration and deviations
Intervals are 95% intervals from a cluster bootstrap over whole trajectories, the unit of dependence, as in the
literature on inference with few clusters (Cameron et al., 2008); resampling seed×trajectory narrows every
interval, as an earlier analysis of ours did before we corrected it (supplement S17). Comparisons at four
trajectories use exact sign-flip tests. Their smallest attainable two-sided p is 1/8, above every Holm level
we use, so no comparison at that size can be called resolved; each is reported with its exact p and as "k of
the four". Multiplicity is controlled with Holm’s procedure within each pre-registered family, and not across
rules. Per-dimension evidence uses permutation nulls that keep the 45 state dimensions together, because
they are not independent.
Decision rules were committed to git before the data that tested them, with timestamps a reader can check;
Appendix E gives each rule, its lead time and its verdict verbatim. Pre-registration in machine learning has
workshops of its own (Bertinetto et al. (2021), Albanie et al. (2022)); we commit each rule before its data
so that the analysis cannot follow the result. One rule failed the discipline: the rule for the control that
adds exact copies of windows reached git 2.9 hours after its runs finished, so we withdrew its claim to have
been pre-registered. Several verdicts went against the original or could not settle the question, and we report
each. The ledger keeps 24 superseded entries: nine claims withdrawn on evidence, eight framings withdrawn,
and the rest early hypotheses closed as housekeeping. Analyses written after the data are labelled post hoc
and never change a verdict. Where our setup departs from the original’s or from a rule, Appendix O and
Appendix E say how.
4 The base paper’s claims
4.1 Training on rollouts against teacher forcing
RWM’s central claim is that training the dynamics model on its own autoregressive rollouts beats training it
on one-step targets (teacher forcing) at long forecast horizons. §2 gives the prior: a long-horizon advantage
is what multi-step training aims at, so the question is whether it holds on episodes the models never saw,
and what happens at one step.
Every episode held out once.We trained both arms on five folds, fixed in advance, each holding out two
episodes so that each of the ten episodes is held out exactly once (2,500 iterations, three seeds per arm per
fold). Over the 20 independent out-of-sample trajectories of all ten episodes, teacher forcing’s relative-L1
error at h = 368 exceeds rollout training’s by +2.2 [+1.4, +3.5], a factor of 5.9: the direction replicates across
folds. All 10 of the 10 episodes favour rollout training (two-sided sign-flip test over the episodes, p = 0.002),
and each fold’s own gap, from +0.66 to +4.0, is positive on each of its four trajectories (descriptive at four;
Figure 1a). That test treats the ten episodes as exchangeable, although the models of different folds share
most of their training episodes, so it is a statement about these ten episodes and this dataset, at its scale
(§3.2). On the held-out pair the teacher-forced arm is worse than the training-mean floor at this horizon
(nRMSE 3.8 against 0.94) and the rollout-trained arm is better (0.54), so the factor compares a model that
works with one that drifts beyond the floors (Appendix F).
At one step the order reverses, on short windows.On 300 non-overlapping 33-row units, the same
arms differ by -0.0090 [-0.013, -0.0053] at h = 1, in favour of teacher forcing, which is unsurprising because
it trains on exactly that target. At the episode level the sign agrees on only 6 of ten episodes (sign-flip p
= 0.20), so the reversal is a short-window result and is not resolved episode by episode. A rule committed
before its runs, on the held-out pair’s 60 independent 33-row units, finds the same sign, -0.019 [-0.031,
-0.0093] (Appendix G).
Supporting evidence from longer training.On the held-out pair, 4 trajectories from two episodes,
three seeds trained for 10,000 iterations give a factor of 4.6 at h = 368, with an interval of [+0.70, +2.3]
on the gap; all 4 trajectories agree in sign, and the exact p of 1/8 is the smallest attainable at four. A
rule committed before those runs, on one seed per arm, found the advantage at h = 368; the three-seed
extension was computed afterwards and carries none of its weight. The advantage grows with the horizon
and is weakest inside the eight steps the model is trained on (Appendix G gives every horizon).

4.2 The configuration claim
The original varies the history length M and the forecast length N and says moderate values trade accuracy
against training time, with (M, N) = (32, 8) as its instance. We tested only the accuracy half, one factor
at a time, at 2,500 iterations on the held-out pair. At that budget two shorter histories and both longer
training forecasts beat the centre, but with 4 trajectories no comparison can be significant after exact tests,
and trained longer the centre passes each of them on some reading, so which setting is most accurate depends
on the budget. The original’s trade-off with training time is not tested, and at our budget the centre is not
shown to be best and no alternative is shown to be better (Appendix H).
4.3 The architecture claim: consistent, but a weak test
The original says RWM beats an MLP, a recurrent state-space model (RSSM) and a transformer. We built

5.3 What an ensemble can see: the shared trunk
Abbas et al. (2020) separate aleatoric noise, parameter uncertainty and model inadequacy. Spread across
members estimates at most the second; to the extent that one-step error is model inadequacy, no spread
across members estimates it. That bears on §5.2 before any trunk is considered.
The trunk is shared. The state pathway is one recurrent trunk plus five small heads, and per member 89%
of the parameters are numerically identical to every other member’s. An autoregressive rollout feeds the
ensemble mean back into one recurrent state, so there is one hidden-state trajectory between them and the
spread is that of five heads reading one vector. RWM-U states the sharing; what we add is its measured
size and consequence. Such members have correlated errors by construction, so their spread cannot express
uncertainty the trunk does not already carry. Randomised prior functions and Bootstrapped DQN are
ensembles of the same kind (Osband et al. (2016), Osband et al. (2018)). RWM-U calls its members a
bootstrap ensemble; per-head resampling exists in the online training path and not in the offline one our
arms follow (Appendix O).

5.4 Does the spread accumulate?
RWM-U’s second version says uncertainty is "effectively propagated over long horizons". Testing that against
accumulated error is legitimate. It is not a test of calibration: at depth the input toσis the model’s own
earlier output, wrong by an amountσnever claimed to describe, so a growing error-to-spread ratio is not a
failure of the penalty’s one-step job (§5.2).
The release feeds every member the ensemble mean through one recurrent state, so its spread at a deep step
is a one-step spread at one shared history. We asked whether rolling each member out on its own predictions
instead makes the spread track accumulated error. At h = 100 it closes a share 0.75 [0.69, 0.82] of the log
gap between spread and error (the log of the error-to-spread ratio, compared before and after the change)
on the released checkpoint (its ten released episodes, 20 trajectories), 0.72 [0.67, 0.76] on the shared-trunk
ensembles of the five folds (20), and 0.71 [0.67, 0.76] on the independent ensembles of the folds. On the
held-out pair’s four trajectories the shared-trunk and independent ensembles close 0.80 and 0.88, descriptive
only (exact p 1/8). At h = 100 the released checkpoint’s±1σcoverage rises from 4.6% to 33% and CRPS
and NLL improve too, so the effect is not only a wider spread, but coverage stays below the 58–78% band
around the target, so it does not close the gap (Figure 1b).
What this settles: "effectively propagated" fails as the release rolls the ensemble out; most of the log gap
closes when the members are rolled out separately, though coverage stays below the band. Per-member
propagation is not the released method, and the release’s own imagination rollout, which propagates one
member per environment, is not tested.
5.5 Why the per-member variance collapses
The follow-up reads the low aleatoric value as reflecting small stochasticity in the environment. The obser-
vation is right and the reading is not. The implemented state loss is squared error on a sample from the
predicted Gaussian, not a likelihood: its expectation is (µ−y)2+σ2, which is minimised atσ= 0 for
anyµ. Nothing in the loss opposes it. The upper bound is built as the lower bound plus a learned gap,
so the lower bound receives no gradient from the bound term and moves only through the state loss. The
objective’s optimum isσ= 0 per step; the fed-back sample is a second pathway we did not isolate, and the
teacher-forced arm, which shares the loss, collapses at the same rate.
We tested it before training. On synthetic data whose known noise varies 25×over the input range, the
released head under the implemented loss sits 22×below the true noise and does not track it, while under
the authors’ unused likelihood branch it recovers the level (ratio 0.98, averaged over seeds). Across the 28
runs at the released width that the fitted rate uses (Figure 2 draws the 75 logged runs of 2,500 iterations)
the log-σrange moves linearly in iteration count, falling under sampled MSE and rising under the likelihood
(Figure 7, Appendix J).

5.6 Ranking
Disagreement ranks error well, and we report it with Spearman’s rank correlation (§3.3), taken between
the scalar the method applies and total absolute error across the pooled (trajectory, step) points up to the
horizon, and at h = 1 across trajectories. On the released checkpoint over all ten released episodes (20
independent trajectories), Spearman is +0.90 at h = 100, and its lead over the forecast step index, a counter
that needs no ensemble, is resolved at every horizon from h = 8 on (at h = 1 the index is constant, so no
comparison with it is defined); at h = 1 Pearson is the higher (+0.99 against +0.83). The rules committed
before these readings used Pearson, and their verdicts keep it.
Does the ensemble beat something free? Against the model’s own predicted step size, whole trajectories
cannot settle the margin at h = 368 under Pearson (+0.14, below the 0.29 that sample can resolve), though
under ranks the margin has an interval above zero (+0.31 [+0.24, +0.40]). A rule committed before its
reading put the same comparison to 70 non-overlapping shorter units at h = 100 on the same checkpoint:
the margin is +0.28 [+0.20, +0.39], resolved on this checkpoint’s ten released episodes and not re-tested at
h = 368. Disagreement keeps real information with step size partialled out, so "disagreement ranks error
well" is supported, and "it ranks better than a free signal" holds on shorter units and under ranks but not
under the pre-registered whole-trajectory test.
On a model we trained, a rule committed before its reading (disagreement against the index on the held-out
pair, four trajectories) was inconclusive: the direction held at every horizon, but the paired interval excluded
zero at 1 of 4 horizons, short of the majority it required. The same comparison on the folds’ 20 trajectories,
committed before its data, is resolved: disagreement leads at 4 of 4 horizons and the paired interval excludes
zero at 4.
5.7 Rescaling per horizon
A constant factor could be absorbed by the penalty’s weight, so the one-step shortfall alone does not show
the penalty mis-sized; what no constant can absorb is that the factor grows with the horizon. One constant
scalar does not repair the spread, because the ratio of error to spread grows: on the released checkpoint the
coverage-matching factor k (§3.3) is 6.6 at h = 1 and 42 at h = 368. A multiplier per horizon is a split-
conformal construction (Angelopoulos and Bates, 2021): aσ-normalised score, one quantile per horizon,
fitted on calibration units and scored on others. Its finite-sample guarantee needs exchangeable calibration

plus one over the number of calibration units plus one, so a ten-point band needs at least nine calibration

Fitted on nine episodes and scored on the tenth, the released checkpoint’s±1σcoverage over all ten released

On episodes the model never saw, the evidence is mixed. The multipliers of our rollout-trained models
calibrate 17 of 36 cells (a cell is one multiplier scored on one held-out unit at one horizon) on the held-out
pair’s 4 independent trajectories, and on the five folds a rule committed before its data finds the per-horizon
multiplier calibrates 72 of 180 cells and finds that it does not restore calibration, while the folds’ pooled±1σ
coverage, reported beside it and not as a reading, is 67% at h = 100, inside the ten-point band around the
target. The verdict stands, and both measurements are the result: pooled across folds the multiplier lands
near the target, cell by cell it often does not, and its guarantee is for the units that fitted it.
6 Defects in the released pipeline
Two defects in the released code and data bear on what precedes; Appendix N gives the rest, and Appendix
O the places where the code departs from the paper’s description.
Unmarked episode boundaries.The data file concatenates ten episodes with a termination column
that is identically zero, so the window builder marks all 9,961 windows valid, including 352 that splice one
episode’s end onto the next one’s start (§3.2). We recover the boundaries from the reset rows and exclude
thosewindows. Trainingonsplicedwindows costs nothing measurablein rollout: over32 cells acontaminated
arm is hurt in 0 and helped in 9, consistent with regularisation and not with harm. The defect remains a
real one on leakage grounds (Appendix N).
The action is one step stale in evaluation.Row t holds the action that produced state t: at every
reset row all twelve actions are exactly zero, which a policy network with biases cannot emit, and which
is what Isaac Lab writes at a reset. Training pairs a state with the action in its own row; evaluation
feeds the action from the previous row, one step stale. Shifting the two action slices of the evaluation loop
(model_training.py:129and:132) aligns it, and our harness does this withaction_offset = 1(src/
score_reference.py:180-190). On the released checkpoint’s all ten released episodes (20 independent
trajectories), the stale action raises relative-L1 error by 55% [26, 93] at h = 1 and 37% [21, 65] at h =
32; from h = 100 the change is not resolved (8.2% [-10, 25] there). On the held-out pair’s 4 trajectories it
raises the error at every horizon reported. Our own models trained under the causal pairing are also hurt
by the stale one (Appendix N). We sent this to the first author before submission and had no reply when
we submitted.
Evaluation on training data.The released evaluation draws its trajectories from the data the checkpoint
may have trained on, and no file records which rows it used (§3.2), so no held-out measurement of the
released checkpoint is possible. For our models we keep the two arenas apart throughout.
The variance state.The checkpoint’s stored variance state cannot be reached from the released initiali-
sation at a constant rate: even at the largest step Adam can take (Kingma and Ba, 2015), 45,737 iterations
would be needed, against the iteration counts that the release, the paper and the checkpoint’s tag give. The
first author’s account, that the release is several revisions removed from the setup that trained it, supplies
a mechanism such as a warm start. This is a documentation gap between a release and a run, not an in-
consistency (Appendix J). The first author also confirmed Eq. 4’s intended quantity and the discard of the
aleatoric term (supplement S5).

7 Lessons and limitations
Lessons for a practitioner.
•Use ensemble disagreement as a ranking signal, price it against free alternatives, and do not read it
as a distance.It ranks error well and better than the forecast step; on the released checkpoint it
is also ahead of the model’s own predicted step size on shorter units and under ranks, though the
linear margin on whole trajectories is not resolved. Its size is wrong at every horizon, including one
step (§5.2, §5.6).
•If you need an interval, rescale per horizon and refit on your own model.On the released checkpoint
this restores one-sigma coverage and not two-sigma; on episodes a model never saw the evidence is
mixed (§5.7).
•Roll ensemble members out separately if you want their spread to track accumulated error.Feeding
every member the ensemble mean does not (§5.4).
•Check that the implemented loss is the described loss.Here the variance has an optimum at zero,
which a reading of the code explained (§5.5).
•Count independent trajectories, report an exact p beside every interval at four, and permute whole
trajectories.Sign counts over coupled state dimensions are not independent trials, and an earlier
draft of this paper made its weakest evidence look strongest that way (§3.4).
•Say which arena a calibration number comes from.Measuring on training data neither flatters nor
worsens one-step calibration resolvably, and flatters it at long horizons (§5.2).
•Anchor a decision rule to the horizon the claim is about.Our first rule was anchored at the training
horizon and pointed at the wrong regime (Appendix G).
Limitations.One robot, one gait and one flat terrain, and one dataset, so "generalisation" here means
across velocity commands only. No policy is trained, so what the miscalibration costs RWM-U’s policies is
untested. The reason it may matter is that MOPO’s penalty is meant to bound one-step error at visited
states, and the spread falls short of the error already at one step (§5.2). A test that needs no policy, of the
penalty against the model’s error in predicted return, could not be run because the released data do not
record the velocity command its reward terms score against. The held-out pair is four trajectories from two
episodes, so no comparison there can be significant; the fold replications reach twenty, but their models share
most training episodes. The released checkpoint’s training rows are not recorded. Our baselines are untuned
readings of the original’s table of shapes, and our data are a small fraction of the reference’s. We apply no
correction across our own 35 pre-registered rules, each reported against the threshold it was committed with.
Whether other descendants of the PETS head inherit theσ= 0 optimum is untested.
8 Conclusion
RWM’s central training claim reproduces on held-out episodes at long horizons and reverses at one step on
short windows; its other two claims get weak tests, and at h = 368 the baselines we built never beat even
the training mean. RWM-U’s ensemble disagreement ranks error correctly and sizes it wrongly: even at one
step every ensemble’s error exceeds its spread, members that share a trunk can disagree only about what it
carries, and the release’s rollout stops the spread accumulating, which rolling members out separately partly
repairs. The per-member variance collapses because the implemented loss is minimised at zero. Rescaling per
horizon restores one-sigma coverage on the released checkpoint, not two-sigma, and on unseen episodes the
evidence is mixed. As a scalar penalty, disagreement is a ranking and not a scale, and a ranking use deserves
validation on the deployment distribution. What should travel is the objective’s property, which should be
checked wherever a boundedσhead is trained against sampled squared error; the released artifact’s specific
factors should be checked rather than assumed, and the limits of one robot’s data are limits on what could
be resolved, not findings in either direction.
