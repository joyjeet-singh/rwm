\begin{abstract}
We reimplement the dynamics model of the Robotic World Model (RWM) and test the uncertainty its follow-up, RWM-U, uses as a reward penalty in offline policy optimisation. Our rebuild matches the release's gradients. RWM's claim that training on its own rollouts, not one-step targets, lowers long-horizon error holds on held-out data: holding each of ten episodes out once, at the original's training length, it wins on every one at the pre-registered horizon; on short windows at one step it loses. RWM-U's ensemble disagreement ranks errors right but sizes them wrong: it ranks them better than the forecast step does (Spearman +0.90 with error), yet in every ensemble measured, error exceeds what the spread implies even at one step. The release feeds every member the ensemble mean, so spread does not track accumulated error: at 100 steps on the released checkpoint one-sigma coverage is 4.6\% against 68\%, and rolling each member on its own predictions raises it to 33\%. Each member's variance collapses: the implemented loss, squared error on a sampled prediction, is minimised at zero. Rescaling the released checkpoint's spread per horizon (fitted on nine episodes, scored on the tenth) restores one-sigma coverage, not two-sigma. We fix two pipeline defects. One robot, gait and dataset; no policy is trained.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Offline model-based reinforcement learning trains a robot's policy inside a learned simulator. The Robotic World Model (RWM; Li, Krause and Hutter) learns such a simulator for a quadruped from recorded robot data, and its follow-up, RWM-U, adds an ensemble of predictors whose disagreement penalises the reward, in the manner of MOPO (Yu et al., 2020). A policy can be trusted inside the simulator only as far as the simulator's errors are known, so the penalty has two jobs: to rank where the model is more likely to be wrong, and to say by how much. We test whether it does either, on the released code and checkpoint and on models we rebuilt from the papers' descriptions.

\textbf{What we found, in plain words.} The rebuild matches the release's gradients before any training, so later differences belong to the method and not to our wiring (\S{}3.1). RWM's central claim, that training on the model's own rollouts lowers long-horizon error more than training on one-step targets (teacher forcing), holds on episodes the models never saw: with each of ten episodes held out once, rollout training wins on every episode at the pre-registered horizon, and on short windows at one step it loses (\S{}4.1, Figure 1a). The other two claims we tested get only weak tests (\S{}4.2, \S{}4.3).

\begin{figure}[!ht]\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{\detokenize{figures/paper_fig7_findings.png}}\caption{(a) Our models, 2,500 iterations: teacher forcing minus rollout, relative L1 at h = 368, by held-out episode (20 trajectories; episode-level exact p 0.0020). (b) Error over spread, released checkpoint and fold ensembles (20 trajectories each); calibrated 0.80; dashed: per-member rollout, not released.}\end{figure}

RWM-U's ensemble disagreement ranks errors correctly and sizes them wrongly, in three separate ways. Even at one step, every ensemble's error exceeds what its spread implies (\S{}5.2). The five members share one trunk, so they can disagree only about what that trunk carries (\S{}5.3). And the release feeds every member the ensemble mean, so the spread does not grow with the horizon as the error does; rolling each member on its own predictions, which is not the released method, partly repairs this (\S{}5.4, Figure 1b). Separately, the per-member variance the method discards collapses because the implemented loss is minimised at zero (\S{}5.5). The spread ranks error better than the forecast step does (\S{}5.6), and rescaling it per horizon restores one-sigma coverage, though not two-sigma, on the released checkpoint; on episodes a model never saw the evidence is mixed (\S{}5.7). We also fix two defects in the released pipeline (\S{}6). Everything is measured on one robot, one gait and one dataset, and no policy is trained, so what the miscalibration costs a policy is not tested (\S{}7).

Figure 1 shows one finding about each original paper. Panel (a) is the base paper's claim that training on rollouts beats teacher forcing, measured on episodes the models never saw: the gap at h = 368, one interval per fold and one point per held-out episode. Panel (b) asks whether the follow-up's spread grows with the horizon as the error does, under the released rollout and under a per-member rollout that is not the released method.

\textbf{Contributions.}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RWM's claims, tested.} Holding each of the ten episodes out once, rollout training beats teacher forcing at 368 steps on every episode and loses at one step on short windows (\S{}4.1). The other two claims get weak tests (\S{}4.2 and \S{}4.3); two pipeline defects get fixes (\S{}6).
\item \textbf{Three gaps between RWM-U's spread and its error.} Even at one step every ensemble's error exceeds its spread; members sharing one trunk can disagree only about what that trunk carries; feeding them the ensemble mean stops the spread accumulating, which per-member rollout partly repairs (\S{}5.2 to \S{}5.4).
\item \textbf{Why the discarded per-member variance collapses.} The implemented loss is squared error on a sampled prediction, not the Gaussian likelihood, so its optimum is $\sigma$ = 0: derived, shown against known synthetic noise, and approached at an ordinary Adam step per iteration (\S{}5.5).
\item \textbf{What the spread is still good for.} It ranks error better than the forecast step (Spearman). Exploratory ranks also put it ahead of the model's step size, which the pre-registered linear test could not resolve (\S{}5.6). On the released checkpoint, per-horizon rescaling fixes one-sigma coverage, not the tails; on episodes a model never saw the evidence is mixed (\S{}5.7).
\end{itemize}

\S{}2 places the work; \S{}3 sets out the data, metrics and statistics; \S{}4 and \S{}5 test the two papers; \S{}6 describes the pipeline defects; \S{}7 gives lessons and limitations; \S{}8 concludes and states what reproduces.


\section{Background and related work}

\textbf{RWM and RWM-U.} RWM (Li, Krause and Hutter) trains a recurrent dynamics model on a quadruped's proprioceptive data and uses it as a simulator for policy learning. RWM-U's ensemble of five predictors shares one state trunk, as its authors state, and penalises the reward by the members' disagreement. The penalty follows MOPO (Yu et al., 2020), which penalises the reward by an ensemble uncertainty estimate, and MOReL (Kidambi et al., 2020), which detects unknown states by pairwise ensemble disagreement; both branch short rollouts from real states, as MBPO does (Janner et al., 2019). As we read MOPO, the penalty is meant to bound the model's one-step error at the states the policy visits. That is the use against which \S{}5.2 measures the spread.

\textbf{Multi-step training.} Training a dynamics model on its own multi-step predictions rather than on one-step targets is an established remedy for compounding error; Venkatraman et al. (2015), Bengio et al. (2015) for sequences, Talvitie (2017) and Lambert et al. (2021) are examples. That sets the prior for \S{}4: a long-horizon advantage for rollout training is what these methods aim at, so the direction of RWM's claim is expected, and the test is whether it holds on held-out episodes of this dataset and what happens at one step.

\textbf{Ensembles that share a trunk.} Deep ensembles are trained from different initialisations and data orderings (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017). Reinforcement-learning ensembles with a shared torso include Bootstrapped DQN (Osband et al., 2016) and randomised prior functions (Osband et al., 2018). That heads sharing a trunk under-report disagreement is established (Lee et al. (2015), Fort, Hu and Lakshminarayanan (2019), Wen et al. (2020) and Havasi et al. (2021)). RWM-U documents its shared trunk; \S{}5.3 asks what the sharing costs.

\textbf{Calibration, recalibration and conformal prediction.} Lu et al. (2022) compare uncertainty heuristics for offline model-based reinforcement learning under a protocol built "to capture the specific covariate shift induced by model-based RL", and report rank and bivariate correlation with true model error separately. That is our distinction between ranking and scale, on the same family of penalties, and the closest precedent. We add coverage measurements on a released checkpoint. Kuleshov et al. (2018) recalibrate regression uncertainty, and Malik et al. (2019) do so for a dynamics model inside model-based reinforcement learning, arguing that "good uncertainties must be calibrated". Our per-horizon rescaling (\S{}5.7) fits a scale on some held-out data and scores it on other held-out data, which resembles split conformal prediction (Angelopoulos and Bates (2021), Stankevičiūtė et al. (2021), Lindemann et al. (2023)); we make no claim of the conformal guarantee.

\textbf{Evaluation statistics.} Henderson et al. (2018) and Agarwal et al. (2021) are the reference points for reporting reinforcement-learning results from few runs. Our intervals rest on few seeds and few trajectories, so \S{}3.4 reports the count beside every interval.

\textbf{The PETS lineage.} The bounded log-$\sigma$ head is inherited from PETS (Chua et al., 2018); Appendix J asks which public descendants inherit its objective as well. Seitzer et al. (2022) show failure modes of $\sigma$ heads trained by maximising likelihood; the released state loss is not a likelihood at all (\S{}5.5).


\section{Setup}

\subsection{The reimplementation}

We rebuilt the dynamics model from the papers' descriptions and the released module: a recurrent (GRU) ensemble whose members predict the next proprioceptive state with a mean head and a bounded log-$\sigma$ head, plus auxiliary heads for contact and termination. The paper describes two loss terms; the implementation has 7. Before any training, our outputs match the released module's bitwise, and our losses and gradients match exactly (a maximum difference of 0.000e+00) across those 7 terms and 106 parameter tensors (Appendix A). A later discrepancy therefore belongs to the method or the data, not to our wiring.

\subsection{Data, arenas and independent units}

The released dataset is 10,000 rows of ANYmal D proprioceptive state and policy actions at 50 Hz, 0.13\% of the data budget the reference trains on: ten concatenated 20-second episodes of 1,000 rows (the first has 999), with a termination column that is identically zero. We recover the episode boundaries from the rows at which every joint velocity and action is zero (supplement S16). Table 1 lists the arenas we measure on, each with its count of mutually non-overlapping 400-step trajectories, which is the sample size.

Two 400-step trajectories whose spans overlap are not independent evidence, so we count only non-overlapping ones, report the count beside every interval, and resample whole trajectories (\S{}3.4). A bootstrap over 4 trajectories has 256 distinct resamples, so its intervals are coarse, and that count bounds every long-horizon claim about the held-out pair.

\textbf{The released checkpoint's training data is not recorded.} No file in either released repository records which rows trained the released checkpoint, and the checkpoint stores no data statistic, hash or path (its tagged iteration is 5,000). Its one-step error is the same on the eight episodes our own model trained on and the two it did not (0.083 against 0.085), where our model's differs (0.075 against 0.12). That is consistent with its having trained on all ten episodes, on some, or on none, and the data cannot tell which. We therefore call its arena its ten released episodes and never call it held out. Everything here ran on two CPU cores (Appendix B).

\subsection{Metrics and floors}

\textbf{Relative-L1} is the reference's own metric, which keeps our numbers comparable to the upstream's printed ones: at each forecast step, the summed absolute error over the state dimensions divided by the summed absolute true state, on normalised states, averaged over trajectories and over the steps after the first 32, which are teacher-forced. Its denominator can pass through zero, so we also report \textbf{normalised RMSE (nRMSE)}: the root of the squared error pooled over the state dimensions, divided by the dimensions' mean standard deviation over the training episodes. A value of one is not the training-mean floor, because that predictor scores the root-mean-square of the dimensions' standard deviations over their mean, which is at least one; we report three floors: predicting the training mean, holding the last state, and repeating a seasonal pattern (\S{}4.3).

\textbf{Coverage at $\pm$k$\sigma$} is the fraction of (trajectory, step, dimension) triples whose absolute error is within k times the predicted $\sigma$; a calibrated Gaussian gives 68\% at $\pm$1$\sigma$ and 95\% at $\pm$2$\sigma$. \textbf{The error-to-spread ratio} $\rho$ is the mean absolute error over the mean predicted $\sigma$; a calibrated Gaussian has $\rho$ = 0.80, so a ratio of one is not calibration, and we report $\rho$ beside that value. \textbf{"Too small by k"} means one thing: the factor k on $\sigma$ that restores nominal $\pm$1$\sigma$ coverage. \textbf{Ranking statements} use Spearman's rank correlation, because a ranking claim is about order and a rank statistic does not depend on scale or on a linear relation; a rule committed with Pearson's correlation keeps it, and we say so (Appendix L gives Kendall's).

Curves are reported at horizons h = 1, 8, 32, 100, 128 and 368, each cumulative from the first forecast step to h. \textbf{h = 100} is the method's own imagination rollout length. \textbf{h = 368} is the upstream's open-loop diagnostic length, 400 minus the 32-step prefix, 3.7$\times$ longer than the method's rollout and not a deployment horizon.

\subsection{Statistical protocol, pre-registration and deviations}

Intervals are 95\% intervals from a cluster bootstrap over whole trajectories, the unit of dependence, as in the literature on inference with few clusters (Cameron et al., 2008); resampling seed $\times$ trajectory narrows every interval, as an earlier analysis of ours did before we corrected it (supplement S17). Comparisons at four trajectories use exact sign-flip tests. Their smallest attainable two-sided p is 1/8, above every Holm level we use, so no comparison at that size can be called resolved; each is reported with its exact p and as "k of the four". Multiplicity is controlled with Holm's procedure within each pre-registered family, and not across rules. Per-dimension evidence uses permutation nulls that keep the 45 state dimensions together, because they are not independent.

Decision rules were committed to git before the data that tested them, with timestamps a reader can check; Appendix E gives each rule, its lead time and its verdict verbatim. Pre-registration in machine learning has workshops of its own (Bertinetto et al. (2021), Albanie et al. (2022)); we commit each rule before its data so that the analysis cannot follow the result. One rule failed the discipline: the rule for the control that adds exact copies of windows reached git 2.9 hours after its runs finished, so we withdrew its claim to have been pre-registered. Several verdicts went against the original or could not settle the question, and we report each. The ledger keeps 24 superseded entries: nine claims withdrawn on evidence, eight framings withdrawn, and the rest early hypotheses closed as housekeeping. Analyses written after the data are labelled post hoc and never change a verdict. Where our setup departs from the original's or from a rule, Appendix O and Appendix E say how.


\section{The base paper's claims}

\subsection{Training on rollouts against teacher forcing}

RWM's central claim is that training the dynamics model on its own autoregressive rollouts beats training it on one-step targets (teacher forcing) at long forecast horizons. \S{}2 gives the prior: a long-horizon advantage is what multi-step training aims at, so the question is whether it holds on episodes the models never saw, and what happens at one step.

\textbf{Every episode held out once.} We trained both arms on five folds, fixed in advance, each holding out two episodes so that each of the ten episodes is held out exactly once (2,500 iterations, three seeds per arm per fold). Over the 20 independent out-of-sample trajectories of all ten episodes, teacher forcing's relative-L1 error at h = 368 exceeds rollout training's by +2.2 [+1.4, +3.5], a factor of 5.9: the direction replicates across folds. All 10 of the 10 episodes favour rollout training (two-sided sign-flip test over the episodes, p = 0.002), and each fold's own gap, from +0.66 to +4.0, is positive on each of its four trajectories (descriptive at four; Figure 1a). That test treats the ten episodes as exchangeable, although the models of different folds share most of their training episodes, so it is a statement about these ten episodes and this dataset, at its scale (\S{}3.2). On the held-out pair the teacher-forced arm is worse than the training-mean floor at this horizon (nRMSE 3.8 against 0.94) and the rollout-trained arm is better (0.54), so the factor compares a model that works with one that drifts beyond the floors (Appendix F).

\textbf{At one step the order reverses, on short windows.} On 300 non-overlapping 33-row units, the same arms differ by -0.0090 [-0.013, -0.0053] at h = 1, in favour of teacher forcing, which is unsurprising because it trains on exactly that target. At the episode level the sign agrees on only 6 of ten episodes (p = 0.20), so the reversal is a short-window result and is not resolved episode by episode. A rule committed before its runs, on the held-out pair's 60 independent 33-row units, finds the same sign, -0.019 [-0.031, -0.0093] (Appendix G).

\textbf{Supporting evidence from longer training.} On the held-out pair, 4 trajectories from two episodes, three seeds trained for 10,000 iterations give a factor of 4.6 at h = 368, with an interval of [+0.70, +2.3] on the gap; all 4 trajectories agree in sign, and the exact p of 1/8 is the smallest attainable at four. A rule committed before those runs, on one seed per arm, found the advantage at h = 368; the three-seed extension was computed afterwards and carries none of its weight. The advantage grows with depth and is weakest inside the eight steps the model is trained on (Appendix G gives every horizon).

\subsection{The configuration claim}

The original varies the history length M and the forecast length N and says moderate values trade accuracy against training time, with (M, N) = (32, 8) as its instance. We tested only the accuracy half, one factor at a time, at 2,500 iterations on the held-out pair. At that budget two shorter histories and both longer training forecasts beat the centre, but with 4 trajectories no comparison can be significant after exact tests, and trained longer the centre passes each of them on some reading, so which setting is most accurate depends on the budget. The original's trade-off with training time is not tested, and at our budget the centre is not shown to be best and no alternative is shown to be better (Appendix H).

\subsection{The architecture claim: consistent, but a weak test}

The original says RWM beats an MLP, a recurrent state-space model (RSSM) and a transformer. We built all three to our reading of the original's table of shapes, gave them RWM's settings, and trained each both teacher-forced, as the original does, and autoregressively. Table 2 gives nRMSE at h = 368. Every baseline is worse than RWM on all four trajectories, but that is the smallest p the sample allows, and no baseline beats even the training mean, which is the stronger of the three floors here. The result is consistent with the claim and a weak test of it: the baselines are untuned, their sizes and settings are our reading, and four trajectories cannot reject anything.

\section{The follow-up's uncertainty}

\subsection{What RWM-U claims and what its code computes}

RWM-U makes three claims about its ensemble that we can test. It says the learned variance "captures aleatoric uncertainty effectively" (its \S{}4.1, unchanged across versions); that the disagreement across members "closely follows the trend of the prediction error" and so serves as a trust metric (its \S{}5.1); and, from its second version on, that uncertainty is "effectively propagated over long horizons".

The released code computes two quantities at each step: the mean across members of each member's predicted $\sigma$, which we call aleatoric, and the standard deviation across the members' mean predictions, which we call epistemic (\texttt{system\_dynamics.py:125-126}). Only the second reaches the policy. It is returned at \texttt{envs/\allowbreak{}base\allowbreak{}.py:158} and applied as a reward penalty with weight $-$1.0 at \texttt{:166}; the aleatoric term is bound to a variable that is never read again (\texttt{:142}). The paper's Eq. 4 specifies a variance where the code computes a standard deviation; the first author confirms that the code's quantity is the intended one and that the aleatoric term is reported for analysis only (supplement S5). We measure the code's quantity. RWM-U does not claim a calibrated interval, and a penalty weight can absorb a constant factor, so what we test is what the spread reports at one step and how that changes with the horizon (\S{}5.2, \S{}5.7). The five members share one recurrent trunk, as RWM-U's authors state, and \S{}5.3 measures what that costs.

As we read MOPO's penalty, its job is to bound the model's error at the states the policy visits, one step at a time. That is the job against which we judge the spread, and it separates three gaps that a single ratio of spread to error runs together: the one-step gap on held-out data (\S{}5.2); the gap between what an ensemble can and cannot see (\S{}5.3); and the gap between a one-step spread and accumulated error (\S{}5.4).

\subsection{At one step, the spread already falls short}

At one step nothing has accumulated: the input is the true state, and $\sigma$ is asked the question it was trained on. R compares squared errors, so it is larger than the ratio of mean absolute error to spread and than the coverage-matching factor k; the three summarise one shortfall.

\textbf{Does measuring on training data flatter the result?} Not at one step. With every episode held out once and also scored in sample, the ratio of in-sample to held-out error-to-spread at h = 1 is 0.92 [0.81, 1.1]: not resolved in either direction. \subsection{What an ensemble can see: the shared trunk}

Abbas et al. (2020) separate aleatoric noise, parameter uncertainty and model inadequacy. Spread across members estimates at most the second; to the extent that one-step error is model inadequacy, no spread across members estimates it. That bears on \S{}5.2 before any trunk is considered.

The trunk is shared. The state pathway is one recurrent trunk plus five small heads, and per member 89\% of the parameters are numerically identical to every other member's. An autoregressive rollout feeds the ensemble mean back into one recurrent state, so there is one hidden-state trajectory between them and the spread is that of five heads reading one vector. RWM-U states the sharing; what we add is its measured size and consequence. Such members have correlated errors by construction, so their spread cannot express uncertainty the trunk does not already carry. Randomised prior functions and Bootstrapped DQN are ensembles of the same kind (Osband et al. (2016), Osband et al. (2018)). RWM-U calls its members a bootstrap ensemble; per-head resampling exists in the online training path and not in the offline one our arms follow (Appendix O).

Training five independently initialised full models and using them as an ensemble improves the overconfidence factor at h = 100 by 2.0$\times$, against a minimum detectable effect (the smallest gain the comparison could reliably detect) of 1.5$\times$, on the held-out pair's 4 independent trajectories; on the folds' 20 trajectories the gain is 1.8$\times$ with 6.0 points more coverage. That bounds the sharing effect and does not isolate it: independent members also differ in capacity (3.5$\times$ the parameters) and in data order, and at matched capacity the gain, 1.8$\times$, is below what that comparison can resolve. Combining independence with the corrected loss adds nothing beyond independence alone (Appendix K). Neither ensemble is an interval.

\subsection{Does the spread accumulate?}

RWM-U's second version says uncertainty is "effectively propagated over long horizons". Testing that against accumulated error is legitimate. It is not a test of calibration: at depth the input to $\sigma$ is the model's own earlier output, wrong by an amount $\sigma$ never claimed to describe, so a growing error-to-spread ratio is not a failure of the penalty's one-step job (\S{}5.2).

The release feeds every member the ensemble mean through one recurrent state, so its spread at a deep step is a one-step spread at one shared history. We asked whether rolling each member out on its own predictions instead makes the spread track accumulated error. At h = 100 it closes a share 0.75 [0.69, 0.82] of the log gap between spread and error (the log of the error-to-spread ratio, compared before and after the change) on the released checkpoint (its ten released episodes, 20 trajectories), 0.72 [0.67, 0.76] on the shared-trunk ensembles of the five folds (20), and 0.71 [0.67, 0.76] on the independent ensembles of the folds. On the held-out pair's four trajectories the shared-trunk and independent ensembles close 0.80 and 0.88, descriptive only (exact p 1/8). At h = 100 the released checkpoint's $\pm$1$\sigma$ coverage rises from 4.6\% to 33\% and CRPS and NLL improve too, so the effect is not only a wider spread, but coverage stays below the 58--78\% band around the target, so it does not close the gap (Figure 1b).

What this settles: "effectively propagated" fails as the release rolls the ensemble out; most of what the members know would propagate if they were rolled out separately. Per-member propagation is not the released method, and the release's own imagination rollout, which propagates one member per environment, is not tested.

\subsection{Why the per-member variance collapses}

The follow-up reads the low aleatoric value as reflecting small stochasticity in the environment. The observation is right and the reading is not. The implemented state loss is squared error on a sample from the predicted Gaussian, not a likelihood: its expectation is ($\mu$ $-$ y)² + $\sigma$², which is minimised at $\sigma$ = 0 for any $\mu$. Nothing in the loss opposes it. The upper bound is built as the lower bound plus a learned gap, so the lower bound receives no gradient from the bound term and moves only through the state loss. The objective's optimum is $\sigma$ = 0 per step; the fed-back sample is a second pathway we did not isolate, and the teacher-forced arm, which shares the loss, collapses at the same rate.

We tested it before training. On synthetic data whose known noise varies 25$\times$ over the input range, the released head under the implemented loss sits 22$\times$ below the true noise and does not track it, while under the authors' unused likelihood branch it recovers the level (ratio 0.98, averaged over seeds). Across the 28 runs at the released width that the fitted rate uses (Figure 2 draws the 75 logged runs of 2,500 iterations) the log-$\sigma$ range moves linearly in iteration count, falling under sampled MSE and rising under the likelihood (Figure 7, Appendix J).

(a) The lower (solid) and upper (dashed) bound of $\sigma$ per logged iteration; 342 saved checkpoints reproduce them. (b) Change in $\log\Delta$ per iteration over the learning rate, with Adam's typical and worst-case bounds; the corrected objective flips the sign.}\end{figure}

Figure 2 shows $\sigma$ itself, and the rate is an Adam property. The collapse moves log $\Delta$, the log of the gap between the $\sigma$ bounds, by a median 0.94 learning rates per iteration over 81 runs, the ordinary step of Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2015), and only the sign flip speaks to the objective. Even at the largest step Adam can take (3.2$\times$ the learning rate), the checkpoint's variance state would need 45,737 iterations from the released initialisation, against the 2,500 we trained for. The bounded head is inherited from PETS (Chua et al., 2018); of 13 public repositories inspected, 10 implement it and 1 optionally trains it on sampled squared error (Appendix J).

\subsection{Ranking}

Disagreement ranks error well, and we report it with Spearman's rank correlation (\S{}3.3), taken between the scalar the method applies and total absolute error across the pooled (trajectory, step) points up to the horizon, and at h = 1 across trajectories. On the released checkpoint over all ten released episodes (20 independent trajectories), Spearman is +0.90 at h = 100, and its lead over the forecast step index, a counter that needs no ensemble, is resolved at every horizon from h = 8 on (at h = 1 the index is constant, so no comparison with it is defined); at h = 1 Pearson is the higher (+0.99 against +0.83). The rules committed before these readings used Pearson, and their verdicts keep it.

Does the ensemble beat something free? Against the model's own predicted step size, whole trajectories cannot settle the margin at h = 368 under Pearson (+0.14, below the 0.29 that sample can resolve), though under ranks the margin has an interval above zero (+0.31 [+0.24, +0.40]). A rule committed before its reading put the same comparison to 70 non-overlapping shorter units at h = 100 on the same checkpoint: the margin is +0.28 [+0.20, +0.39], resolved on this checkpoint's ten released episodes and not re-tested at h = 368. Disagreement keeps real information with step size partialled out, so "disagreement ranks error well" is supported and "the ensemble is needed to rank error" is not.

On a model we trained, a rule committed before its reading (disagreement against the index on the held-out pair, four trajectories) was inconclusive: the direction held at every horizon, but the paired interval excluded zero at 1 of 4 horizons, short of the majority it required. The same comparison on the folds' 20 trajectories, committed before its data, is resolved: disagreement leads at 4 of 4 horizons and the paired interval excludes zero at 4.

\subsection{Rescaling per horizon}

A constant factor would be absorbed by the penalty's weight, so the shortfall at one step is not the finding; what no constant can absorb is that the factor grows with the horizon. One constant scalar does not repair the spread, because the ratio of error to spread grows: on the released checkpoint the coverage-matching factor k (\S{}3.3) is 6.6 at h = 1 and 42 at h = 368. A multiplier per horizon is a split-conformal construction (Angelopoulos and Bates, 2021): a $\sigma$-normalised score, one quantile per horizon, fitted on calibration units and scored on others. Its finite-sample guarantee needs exchangeable calibration units, which here means episodes and not steps, and then coverage lies between the target and the target plus one over the number of calibration units plus one, so a ten-point band needs at least nine calibration episodes. The units of the nine-episode fit are the released checkpoint's ten released episodes; if it trained on them, which the data cannot rule out, its guarantee holds for in-sample use only.

Fitted on nine episodes and scored on the tenth, the released checkpoint's $\pm$1$\sigma$ coverage over all ten released episodes' 20 independent trajectories is within the band at every horizon. $\pm$2$\sigma$ is not restored: at h = 100 it is 84\% [81, 86] against 95\%, because the errors are heavy-tailed (across all 48 cells of the tails analysis, 30 over-cover at $\pm$1$\sigma$ and under-cover at $\pm$2$\sigma$ once $\sigma$ is rescaled). CRPS improves, from 0.66 to 0.58 at h = 100. Rescaling fixes the scale and not the shape. An earlier reading that every cell is Gaussian was withdrawn (Appendices M and E).

On episodes the model never saw, the evidence is mixed. The multipliers of our rollout-trained models calibrate 17 of 36 cells (a cell is one multiplier scored on one held-out unit at one horizon) on the held-out pair's 4 independent trajectories, and on the five folds a rule committed before its data finds the per-horizon multiplier calibrates 72 of 180 cells and finds that it does not restore calibration, while the folds' pooled $\pm$1$\sigma$ coverage, reported beside it and not as a reading, is 67\% at h = 100, inside the ten-point band around the target. Both readings are the result: pooled across folds the multiplier lands near the target, cell by cell it often does not, and its guarantee is for the units that fitted it.


\section{Defects in the released pipeline}

Two defects in the released code and data bear on what precedes; Appendix N gives the rest, and Appendix O the places where the code departs from the paper's description.

\textbf{Unmarked episode boundaries.} The data file concatenates ten episodes with a termination column that is identically zero, so the window builder marks all 9,961 windows valid, including 352 that splice one episode's end onto the next one's start (\S{}3.2). We recover the boundaries from the reset rows and exclude those windows. Training on spliced windows costs nothing measurable in rollout: over 32 cells a contaminated arm is hurt in 0 and helped in 9, consistent with regularisation and not with harm. The defect remains a real one on leakage grounds (Appendix N).

\textbf{The action is one step stale in evaluation.} Row t holds the action that produced state t: at every reset row all twelve actions are exactly zero, which a policy network with biases cannot emit, and which is what Isaac Lab writes at a reset. Training pairs a state with the action in its own row; evaluation feeds the action from the previous row, one step stale. Shifting the two action slices of the evaluation loop (\texttt{model\_training.py:129} and \texttt{:132}) aligns it, and our harness does this with \texttt{action\_offset = 1} (\texttt{src/\allowbreak{}score\_\allowbreak{}reference\allowbreak{}.py:180-190}). On the released checkpoint's all ten released episodes (20 independent trajectories), the stale action raises relative-L1 error by 55\% [26, 93] at h = 1 and 37\% [21, 65] at h = 32; from h = 100 the change is not resolved (8.2\% [-10, 25] there). On the held-out pair's 4 trajectories it raises the error at every horizon reported. Our own models trained under the causal pairing are also hurt by the stale one (Appendix N). We sent this to the first author before submission and had no reply when we submitted.

\textbf{Evaluation on training data.} The released evaluation draws its trajectories from the data the checkpoint may have trained on, and no file records which rows it used (\S{}3.2), so no held-out measurement of the released checkpoint is possible. For our models we keep the two arenas apart throughout.

\textbf{The variance state.} The checkpoint's stored variance state cannot be reached from the released initialisation at a constant rate: even at the largest step Adam can take (Kingma and Ba, 2015), 45,737 iterations would be needed, against the iteration counts that the release, the paper and the checkpoint's tag give. The first author's account, that the release is several revisions removed from the setup that trained it, supplies a mechanism such as a warm start. This is a documentation gap between a release and a run, not an inconsistency (Appendix J). The first author also confirmed Eq. 4's intended quantity and the discard of the aleatoric term (supplement S5).


\section{Lessons and limitations}

\textbf{Lessons for a practitioner.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Use ensemble disagreement as a ranking signal, price it against free alternatives, and do not read it as a distance.} It ranks error well and better than the forecast step; the model's own predicted step size ranks nearly as well on whole trajectories, and disagreement is ahead of it on shorter units on the released checkpoint. Its size is wrong at every horizon, including one step (\S{}5.2, \S{}5.6).
\item \emph{If you need an interval, rescale per horizon and refit on your own model.} On the released checkpoint this restores one-sigma coverage and not two-sigma; on episodes a model never saw the evidence is mixed (\S{}5.7).
\item \emph{Roll ensemble members out separately if you want their spread to track accumulated error.} Feeding every member the ensemble mean does not (\S{}5.4).
\item \emph{Check that the implemented loss is the described loss.} Here the variance has an optimum at zero, which a reading of the code explained (\S{}5.5).
\item \emph{Count independent trajectories, report an exact p beside every interval at four, and permute whole trajectories.} Sign counts over coupled state dimensions are not independent trials, and an earlier draft of this paper made its weakest evidence look strongest that way (\S{}3.4).
\item \emph{Say which arena a calibration number comes from.} Measuring on training data neither flatters nor worsens one-step calibration resolvably, and flatters it at long horizons (\S{}5.2).
\item \emph{Anchor a decision rule to the horizon the claim is about.} Our first rule was anchored at the training horizon and pointed at the wrong regime.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations.} One robot, one gait and one flat terrain, and one dataset, so "generalisation" here means across velocity commands only. No policy is trained, so what the miscalibration costs RWM-U's policies is untested. The reason it may matter is that MOPO's penalty is meant to bound one-step error at visited states, and the spread falls short of the error already at one step (\S{}5.2). A test that needs no policy, of the penalty against the model's error in predicted return, could not be run because the released data do not record the velocity command its reward terms score against. The held-out pair is four trajectories from two episodes, so no comparison there can be significant; the fold replications reach twenty, but their models share most training episodes. The released checkpoint's training rows are not recorded. Our baselines are untuned readings of the original's table of shapes, and our data are a small fraction of the reference's. We apply no correction across our own 28 pre-registered rules, each reported against the threshold it was committed with. Whether other descendants of the PETS head inherit the $\sigma$ = 0 optimum is untested.


\section{Conclusion}

RWM's central training claim reproduces on held-out episodes at long horizons and reverses at one step on short windows; its other two claims get weak tests, and the baselines we built never beat even the training mean. RWM-U's ensemble disagreement ranks error correctly and sizes it wrongly: even at one step every ensemble's error exceeds its spread, members that share a trunk can disagree only about what it carries, and the release's rollout stops the spread accumulating, which rolling members out separately partly repairs. The per-member variance collapses because the implemented loss is minimised at zero. Rescaling per horizon restores one-sigma coverage on the released checkpoint, not two-sigma, and on unseen episodes the evidence is mixed. As a scalar penalty, disagreement is a ranking and not a scale, and a ranking use deserves validation on the deployment distribution. What should travel is the objective's property, which should be checked wherever a bounded $\sigma$ head is trained against sampled squared error; the released artifact's specific factors should be checked rather than assumed, and the limits of one robot's data are limits on what could be resolved, not findings in either direction.

\section{Statistical methods}

The procedures behind every interval and test in the paper (\S{}3.4), stated once.

\textbf{Cluster bootstrap over trajectories.} Each 95\% interval resamples whole 400-step trajectories, the unit of dependence, with replacement and recomputes the statistic inside every draw; seeds, where pooled, are pooled inside each draw. With 4 trajectories there are 256 distinct resamples, so such intervals are coarse and the percentile method is unreliable; the literature on inference with few clusters is the reason (Cameron et al. (2008)), and we pair each interval at that size with an exact test. Resampling pooled seed $\times$ trajectory values instead of trajectories narrows intervals by a mean 1.42$\times$ and changes 1 of 16 verdicts, in the out-of-sample h = 8 cell at 200-step trajectories and 2,500 iterations; wherever the two units differ we report both.

\textbf{Exact sign-flip tests.} For a paired difference over n trajectories the statistic is the mean difference and the null makes each trajectory's sign equally likely; every sign pattern is enumerated and the p-value is two-sided. At four trajectories the smallest attainable p is 0.125, above the first Holm level of the configuration sweep (0.00625) and of the baseline and size-matched families, so none of those families can reject anything; 154 of the 228 comparisons at four trajectories reach that minimum. At the episode level, at h = 368, the ten episodes give a smallest p of 0.0020.

\textbf{Multiplicity.} Holm's procedure holds the chance of any false rejection at the five per cent level within each pre-registered family. No correction is applied across rules.

\textbf{Permutation nulls that keep dimensions together.} The 45 state dimensions of a robot are coupled and share a forecast-depth trend, so a null that treats them as independent trials is wrong. Where we test a per-dimension correlation, the null permutes whole trajectories, which preserves the coupling between dimensions; Appendix I gives the tests and the binomial version they replace.

\section{Metrics and floors}

Definitions of the metrics and floors (\S{}3.3), the two nRMSE aggregations and the check on the coverage nominal.

\textbf{The metrics' formulas (\S{}3.1).}

\textbf{Relative-L1} is the reference's own metric, reproduced in behaviour (\texttt{model\_training.py:203}) so that our numbers are comparable to the upstream's printed one. On config-normalised states, per forecast step,

\[r_t \;=\; \frac{\sum_{d=1}^{45}\bigl|\hat{s}_{t,d}-s_{t,d}\bigr|}{\sum_{d=1}^{45}\bigl|s_{t,d}\bigr|}\]

and the reported figure is the flat mean over trajectories and steps,

\[e \;=\; \frac{1}{B\,(T-t_0)}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{t=t_0+1}^{T} r_{t}^{(b)}\]

with $t_0$ = \texttt{history\_horizon} = 32: the first 32 steps are teacher-forced and excluded. The denominator is recomputed at every step as a 45-term sum in normalised space, so it can pass through zero, which is why this metric goes non-finite on low-dimensional state groups and why a second one exists.

\textbf{Normalised RMSE} fixes the denominator once, over the training episodes only:

\[\mathrm{nRMSE}_t \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{45}\sum_{d=1}^{45}\mathrm{MSE}_{t,d}}}{\dfrac{1}{45}\sum_{d=1}^{45}\sigma^{\mathrm{tr}}_{d}}\quad\text{with}\quad \mathrm{MSE}_{t,d}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\bigl(\hat{s}^{(b)}_{t,d}-s^{(b)}_{t,d}\bigr)^{2}\]

where the scale constant is

\[\sigma^{\mathrm{tr}}_{d} \;=\; \operatorname{sd}\bigl(\{\,\tilde{s}_{i,d}\;:\; \mathrm{episode}(i)\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{train}}\,\}\bigr)\]

computed once, stored in \texttt{results/\allowbreak{}step4\_\allowbreak{}0a\_\allowbreak{}results\allowbreak{}.json}, and never recomputed per step or derived from held-out data. A value of 1.0 means no better than predicting the training mean. \textbf{The aggregation is form 1}: pool the per-dimension mean squared errors, then divide, a ratio of means. A mean of per-dimension ratios gives whichever dimension has the smallest scale unbounded leverage; the rest of this appendix gives both forms and the comparison the second one inverts.

\textbf{Coverage at $\pm$k$\sigma$} is the fraction of scalar (trajectory, forecast step, state dimension) triples whose absolute realised error falls within k times the $\sigma$ predicted for that same triple:

\[\mathrm{cov}_{\pm k\sigma}(h) \;=\; \frac{1}{B\,h\,45}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{t=t_0+1}^{t_0+h}\sum_{d=1}^{45}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\;\frac{\bigl|\hat{s}^{(b)}_{t,d}-s^{(b)}_{t,d}\bigr|}{\sigma^{(b)}_{t,d}} \;\le\; k \;\right]\]

It is pooled over all three axes with equal weight per triple. It is \textbf{cumulative} over steps 1..h: coverage "at h" averages the whole rollout up to h and is not the value at step h, and every horizon-indexed quantity in this paper follows the same convention. Because it is built from an \emph{absolute} error, $z \le k$ is the two-sided event, so the calibrated targets are $\mathrm{erf}(k/\sqrt{2})$: \textbf{68.27\%} at $\pm$1$\sigma$ and \textbf{95.45\%} at $\pm$2$\sigma$. \textbf{That nominal is checked rather than assumed.} Rescaling each model's $\sigma$ by the single constant that makes mean$|$error$|$ / mean $\sigma$ equal a calibrated Gaussian's 0.7979 lands coverage above the rule's heavy-tail bound of 63.27\% in every one of 48 model $\times$ horizon cells, with 0 below it (15 within its band around 68.27\% and 33 above it; \texttt{M-65}), so the shortfalls reported below are a property of the scale and not of the tail.

\textbf{The overconfidence factor} is how many times larger the typical realised error is than the typical predicted $\sigma$:

\[\rho(h) \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{mean}_{b,t\le h,d}\bigl|\hat{s}^{(b)}_{t,d}-s^{(b)}_{t,d}\bigr|}{\operatorname{mean}_{b,t\le h,d}\ \sigma^{(b)}_{t,d}}\]

It too is a \textbf{ratio of means}, because a mean of ratios is unbounded whenever a single $\sigma$ approaches zero, which is exactly the regime \S{}5.5 puts these models in. $\rho = 1$ is \emph{not} calibration: a calibrated Gaussian has mean$|$error$|$ / $\sigma$ = $\sqrt{2/\pi}$ = 0.7979. So $\rho$ is reported as a magnitude of miscalibration and coverage as the calibrated reading, and both appear everywhere.

\textbf{The two aggregations.} \S{}3.1 states that this paper uses \textbf{form 1}: pool the per-dimension mean squared errors across the 45 state dimensions, then divide by the pooled scale --- a ratio of means. \textbf{Form 2} is the mean of per-dimension ratios.

The two differ because the state dimensions differ widely in scale. Form 2 gives whichever dimension has the smallest denominator unbounded leverage over the aggregate, and a dimension that is nearly constant in the training episodes has a very small denominator. Form 1 has no such lever: a dimension contributes in proportion to its share of the total squared error.

The definition in force throughout the paper is the one \S{}3.1 gives. Nothing in \S{}4.1, \S{}5 or \S{}6 uses form 2.

\textbf{The data budget (\S{}3.2).} \S{}3.2 keeps two sentences of the following.

The base paper's headline is a sample-efficiency result: policies transfer to hardware from 6,000,000 state transitions of world-model pretraining against \textasciitilde{}250M for the model-free baseline (Table I). That is a claim about policy learning and hardware, which we cannot test, but its \emph{world-model} half is a quantity we can count exactly.

\textbf{Our arms consume 7,991 distinct state transitions}: the 7,999 rows of the eight training episodes less one per episode (8 of them), since an episode's first row has no predecessor, a transition being a consecutive pair of rows inside one episode. It is deliberately not the 7,687 training windows, which overlap almost completely (consecutive 40-row windows start one row apart), nor the 640,000 window draws a 2,500-iteration run makes, which resample the same data with replacement. Against the reference's 6,000,000, that is \textbf{751$\times$ less data, 0.133\% of its world-model budget}.

A dynamics model trained on 0.133\% of the reference's data still reproduces the training result, 4.61$\times$ at h = 368 and 2.58$\times$ at h = 100 over 3 seeds (the rule ran on seed 1), and still beats the hold-last floor, by 2.8$\times$ and 2.0$\times$ at those two horizons. That is what this paper can add to the sample-efficiency question without training a policy.

\textbf{Three limits.} It is not a reproduction of the 6,000,000-against-250M comparison, which is about policy learning. It says nothing about whether a policy trained inside our model would transfer to hardware, or anywhere. And our model is evaluated on the narrow distribution it trained on (one robot, one gait, one terrain, velocity commands from a single bounded box), where the reference's 6,000,000 transitions span considerably more; a smaller data budget buys less than it appears to when the evaluation distribution shrinks with it.

\textbf{The permutation tests.} \textbf{\S{}5.6's P column is a permutation P, not a binomial one}, and the binomial P-values an earlier draft attached to its counts are withdrawn (\texttt{S-15}). A binomial null treats the 45 state dimensions as independent trials, and they are not: position, velocity and torque for the same joint are physically coupled, and base linear and angular velocity are coupled through the gait. More importantly, error grows with rollout depth in every trajectory, so \emph{any} $\sigma$ that also grows with depth correlates with \emph{any} trajectory's error, including one it was never paired with.

We therefore permute whole trajectories. The null pairs each trajectory's $\sigma$ with a different trajectory's realised error, which keeps both marginal distributions and the entire cross-dimension dependence structure and destroys only the association under test. The correction is large, and largest exactly where we leaned hardest. The worst-affected cell is teacher-forced armB at h=368, in the in-sample arena. At h = 368 it moves from 5.68e-14 to 0.5565, a factor of about 10\textsuperscript{13}, because under a null that keeps the dependence a random re-pairing already yields 43.8 of 45 dimensions positive on average, so observing 45 of 45 is close to unremarkable. A fair coin centres the count at 22.5 of 45; the dependence-preserving null centres it between 5.1 and 43.8 depending on model, horizon and arena.

\textbf{The larger arenas agree with each other against the smallest.} At n\_independent = 4 400-step trajectories on the held-out pair, in-sample for the checkpoint, the epistemic ordering looks strongest at long horizon (0.0417 at h=128, 0.0435 at h=368) and unremarkable at short (0.4348 at h=1). Both larger arenas invert that. In sample (n\_independent = 16): 0.0052 at h=1, 0.0070 at h=8, against 0.3794 at h=128. Over all ten episodes (n\_independent = 20): 0.0056, 0.0069 and 0.3762, with h = 100 at 0.2769, between h=32's 0.0344 and h=128's 0.3762. Two larger arenas at four and five times the sample say the effect is strongest at \emph{short} horizon, though they are not independent: all 16 in-sample trajectories are among the 20.

The null means explain why, and the explanation is the one that motivates \S{}5.6. At long horizon the shared forecast-depth trend lifts the null to 41.6 of 45 at h = 128, so a count of 45 is close to what chance alone delivers; at h = 1 the null sits near 15.4 and the same count is genuinely surprising. The out-of-sample arena is not wrong so much as blind: at 4 trajectories its smallest attainable P-value is 0.04167, so it cannot tell a strong effect from a marginal one at any horizon. \textbf{That blindness belongs to the 400-step unit, not to the arena}: a 33-row unit gives 60 independent units on the same two episodes, and 14 at h = 100 (rule M-64, Appendix E). We did not recompute this permutation family at the shorter unit, so what the short units change is the scope of the design claim, not any verdict: the arena is underpowered at h = 368, which needs the full 400 rows, and is no longer demonstrably so at h = 128 and below. Running it there is one pass over stored rollouts, and we did not do it. The small arena's numbers are reported because it is the only arena out-of-sample for our own arms, not because it is the better measurement.

\textbf{Nothing here survives multiplicity correction, in any of the three arenas.} Holm--Bonferroni over each arena's 30 model $\times$ horizon cells at $\alpha$ = 0.05 rejects 0 out of sample, 0 in sample and 0 over all ten episodes. Out of sample that is a property of the design: with 4 independent trajectories the smallest attainable P-value is 0.04167, which already exceeds the smallest Holm threshold 0.001667, so no effect of any size could have been rejected there. In sample the miss is real: the smallest P in the family is teacher-forced armB h=128 at 0.0027 against a threshold of 0.001667.


\section{Why the per-member $\sigma$ collapses}

Evidence behind \S{}5.5: the synthetic-noise test, the term-by-term gradient check, the variance-state arithmetic and the PETS lineage with the survey counts.

\textbf{\S{}5.5's permutation detail on the corrected objective.} Under the trajectory permutation test of \S{}5.6, at h = 368, those counts give P = 0.0417 and 1.0000 out of sample, 0.0240 and 0.8746 in sample. The faithful arm's ordering is the one result in this family that points the same way in both arenas; it is also the weakest effect of the three, and it does not survive multiplicity correction either.

\textbf{\S{}5.5's derivation says the collapse happens on any dataset, and that is testable.} It matters because it is what answers the follow-up's own explanation: on the released CSV, "small stochasticity in the environment" and our reading are observationally identical, since the data may simply be nearly deterministic. So under a rule committed before the runs (rule M-50, Appendix E) we built data where it is not.

Synthetic data whose true noise level is \textbf{known} and varies by a factor of 25 across the input range, with a non-constant true mean; the \textbf{same} bounded log-$\sigma$ head as the released model --- \texttt{MLPStateHead} unmodified, including the double-softplus clamp, the learnable \texttt{state\_min\_logstd} and \texttt{state\_log\_delta\_logstd}, and the bound loss at its configured weight --- trained under each objective in turn on 4,000 points for 12,000 iterations at 3 seeds. Nothing else differs between the arms.

\emph{Two ways this is not the released setting.} The head is built here over a \textbf{one-dimensional} state with no recurrent trunk in front of it, where the released one predicts 45 dimensions from a GRU. The trunk's absence is deliberate: the question is about the head's objective, and a GRU would add a confound. The dimensionality matters because the state loss sums over state dimensions, so at one dimension it is roughly 45$\times$ smaller relative to the bound term than in the released path. That makes this setting \emph{more} favourable to $\sigma$ surviving, and the collapse happens anyway.

\textbf{The variance-state arithmetic.} \S{}6 states the conclusion. The arithmetic and the five assumptions it rests on are in \texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}APPENDIX\_\allowbreak{}G\_\allowbreak{}VARIANCE\_\allowbreak{}ARITHMETIC\allowbreak{}.md}, shipped as supplementary, kept out of the body because the numbered claim it once supported is retracted (\texttt{S-19}) and because a forensic case the section then defuses with the author's own reply is not what a reader needs in the body of a reproduction.

So the finding is not that the release is internally inconsistent. It is that \textbf{the released artifacts do not reproduce the released checkpoint's variance state, and the author's account is that the released repository is not the one that trained it.} That is a \textbf{documentation gap between a release and a run}: common, worth recording, and much less interesting than an inconsistency. The $\sigma$ collapse is linear in iteration count and its rate is nearly identical across our runs (\S{}5.5), which makes it a clock, and read as a clock it puts the checkpoint's variance state out of reach of a constant-rate run from the released initialisation at the configured learning rate, at every iteration count the release, the paper and the checkpoint tag state. The first author's account is that the released repository is several revisions removed from the setup that trained the checkpoint, which supplies a mechanism, a warm start or a different \texttt{log\_delta\_logstd} initialisation, that would explain it with no inconsistency at all. We report the arithmetic because it is what let us detect the gap at all, not as a charge against the work.


\textbf{Where the parameterisation comes from.} The bounded log-$\sigma$ head that \S{}5.5 shows has its optimum at $\sigma$ = 0 is largely not this codebase's invention: its clamp is inherited, line for line, from the probabilistic ensembles of Chua, Calandra, McAllister and Levine (PETS, NeurIPS 2018). PETS's Appendix A.1 gives

\begin{verbatim}
logvar = max_logvar - softplus(max_logvar - logvar)
logvar = min_logvar + softplus(logvar - min_logvar)
\end{verbatim}

and \texttt{architectures/\allowbreak{}mlp\allowbreak{}.py:92-93} is those two lines in log-standard-deviation rather than log-variance, with \texttt{system\_dynamics.py:302} supplying PETS's regulariser on the bounds.

What is \textbf{not} inherited is the objective, and one line of the bounds. PETS uses "the negative log prediction probability as our loss function", and that likelihood's log-$\sigma$ term opposes $\sigma$ → 0; \texttt{system\_dynamics.py:283} substitutes squared error on a reparameterised sample, which has no such term. And \texttt{architectures/\allowbreak{}mlp\allowbreak{}.py:91} builds the upper bound as the floor plus a learned positive gap, where PETS keeps the two bounds independent. \S{}5.5 needs both changes: the substitution removes the log-$\sigma$ term, and the tie is why the bound regulariser does not take its place, since the floor cancels out of it. In a descendant that kept PETS's independent bounds, the same regulariser pushes the floor up while the objective pulls $\sigma$ down onto it, a case \S{}5.5 does not derive and we have not tested (ledger \texttt{M-72}). So \textbf{a descendant of this lineage that replaced the likelihood with a sampled squared error, and left nothing pushing its variance floor back up, would inherit the same optimum.} That is a hypothesis about mechanism, untested in any other descendant (\S{}7).

\textbf{How often the substitution is made, which narrows the hypothesis.} Of 10 public repositories examined on 20 September 2026 under the protocol in \texttt{results/\allowbreak{}q1\_\allowbreak{}search\_\allowbreak{}protocol\allowbreak{}.md}, 10 carry the construction and 1 of those trains it against a sampled squared error, and that one only in an optional value-aware mode; the other 9 keep PETS's likelihood (\texttt{results/\allowbreak{}q1\_\allowbreak{}pets\_\allowbreak{}descendants\allowbreak{}.json}). The protocol counts a repository only if it has the bounded head, learnable bounds, and a loss that squares the error of a \emph{sampled} prediction; squaring the error of the predicted \emph{mean}, an option several of these repositories offer, leaves $\sigma$ untrained rather than driving it to zero, and does not count. We read "learnable" as "trainable by the code, by default or not", a reading settled after the survey, because in \texttt{mbrl-lib} and in \texttt{va\_mbpo}, the one repository that inherits, the bounds train only when a caller switches that on (ledger \texttt{M-73}). The survey did not check how each repository treats its variance floor, so 1 of 10 is an upper bound on how often both conditions hold. The survey looked at 13 in all; the other 3 lack the construction, so the protocol does not count them as examined, among them mainline \texttt{rsl\_rl}: the bounded head exists in the fork this paper pins, not in the library it forks, and we do not count the fork, since counting what \S{}5.5 measured as evidence that the result travels would be circular. The protocol capped the survey at 25 repositories; it stopped at 10 examined, 13 looked at, and its notes give no reason. We wrote the protocol before the search, but neither it nor its hash reached git before the results did, so that order rests on our own record; it is a search protocol, not one of Appendix E's decision rules. Search engines rank and truncate, so this is a sample of convenience, but on it the substitution is rare, and a reader who takes the hypothesis to reach widely infers more than the evidence supports.


\section{The shared trunk and independent ensembles}

The shared trunk behind \S{}5.3, from the source and for our own arms, and the two pre-registered comparisons that test it: independent members, and independent members with the corrected objective.

\textbf{Source walkthrough.} \texttt{system\_dynamics.py:34} builds \textbf{one} \texttt{state\_base}. \texttt{system\_dynamics.py:35-41} replicates the \emph{heads} \texttt{ensemble\_size} times, and only the heads. In the forward pass \texttt{system\_dynamics.py:87} evaluates the trunk \textbf{once} and \texttt{:90} hands the identical feature vector to every head; \texttt{system\_dynamics.py:126} then computes the epistemic term as the standard deviation across those heads.

\textbf{Why \S{}5.6's paired test, not overlapping intervals.} Comparing the two marginal intervals for overlap is the wrong comparison: both correlations are measured on the \emph{same} trajectories, so their sampling errors move together and the marginal intervals are needlessly conservative. Two qualifications go with that. The within-rollout effect is \textbf{materially smaller than the pooled figure}, +0.419 against +0.605, so a practitioner should expect disagreement to separate \emph{rollouts} better than it separates \emph{moments within a rollout}. And it is \textbf{not established at short horizon}: r\_dd's interval excludes zero at h=100, h=128, h=368 and spans zero at h=8 and h=32, where too few steps exist to demean against. At h = 100 it is established, at +0.385 [+0.283, +0.701].

\textbf{The h = 1 figure survives the same test, but it is not what it looked like.} At h = 1 the panel has one column, so +0.994 is a correlation over 20 \emph{trajectory-level} points and nothing within a rollout is being tested. Disagreement correlates +0.006 with commanded speed and +0.027 with per-episode difficulty, and partialling both out of the disagreement--error correlation leaves +0.995: it does not move, so the figure is real and not a difficulty artifact. It is nevertheless a statement about \textbf{ranking whole rollouts at one step ahead}, on 20 points, and \S{}7 states it that way.

\textbf{Does it hold on a model we trained?} Everything else in \S{}5.6 is measured on the released checkpoint, because our main arms run at ensemble size 1 where the epistemic term is identically zero. We therefore trained three Arm A arms at \textbf{ensemble size 5}, at 2,500 training iterations and identical in every other setting, under a rule committed to git before the runs existed (rule M-43, Appendix E). It asked that disagreement lead the index at every horizon, and that the paired difference exclude zero at a majority of them.

\textbf{It returns DOES NOT GENERALISE.} The first condition passes completely: disagreement leads the index in \textbf{12 of 12} seed-horizon cells, every paired estimate positive, +0.204 to +0.545. The second fails: the paired difference excludes zero at 1 of 4 horizons, not a majority. We report the verdict the rule returns and do not rewrite the rule, nor its denominator: it was committed over 4 horizons, and adding h = 100 after the fact would change what "a majority" means in a discharged rule. \S{}5.6's released-checkpoint table follows the six-horizon grid, because no pre-registration is stated over it, so the two counts are deliberately different.

\textbf{What separates the two conditions is sample size, and we measured that rather than asserting it.} Our own arms can only be scored out-of-sample on the held-out pair, n\_independent = 4, where the released checkpoint's finding used 20. Subsampling four trajectories at a time from a twenty-trajectory pool, the rule's criterion fires on 75\% of draws on average and on only 24\% at h=8. That estimate is an \textbf{upper bound}, because the pool it subsamples is in-sample for these arms, where the effect is +0.425 to +0.790 against +0.204 to +0.545 on the held-out pair. So the rule was under-powered at the sample size it faced, decisively at one horizon. We do not claim it could not have passed, only that it was committed without checking what it could detect: a failure of ours, the same one the ledger records as M-24, a rule anchored without regard to the regime it would be applied in.

\emph{Reported as a companion and not as a discharge:} on all ten episodes (n\_independent = 20 400-step trajectories, \textbf{in-sample} for these arms, which trained on eight of them) the same measurement excludes zero at 4 of 4 horizons and would have satisfied both conditions. It cannot discharge the rule, which is stated over the out-of-sample arena; we record it only so the comparison with the released checkpoint's 20 is like for like.

\emph{Why the within-step control is undefined for the forecast index.} The within-step control holds the forecast step fixed and correlates across trajectories, so it annihilates any quantity that is constant across trajectories at a fixed step --- which is the forecast index, whose within-step correlation is therefore undefined rather than zero. It does \textbf{not} annihilate a per-trajectory scalar like \texttt{entry-res}, which varies across exactly the axis the control varies over. Rule M-51 said the opposite, and \texttt{M-54} records the correction.


Rule X6 was committed and pushed before its readings existed (ledger M-88; Appendix E gives its lead time) and is discharged in M-91. It is exploratory and re-opens no rule: rules M-51 and M-52 stand. It puts \S{}5.6's comparison to more units. The released checkpoint's ten episodes are cut into non-overlapping units of the model's history plus h forecast steps, inside each episode, by rule M-64's index builder, at h = 32 and h = 100, which governs. The statistic is rules M-51 and M-52's margin, r(disagreement, $|$error$|$) $-$ r(step size, $|$error$|$) on the applied scalar, a Pearson correlation over every (unit, step) cell, imported from their script; its 95\% interval resamples whole units, paired, 20,000 times (seed 0). The rule reads \textbf{DISAGREEMENT BEATS STEP SIZE} if the interval's lower bound is above zero, STEP SIZE BEATS DISAGREEMENT if its upper bound is below zero, and NOT RESOLVED otherwise. \section{Defects in the released pipeline}

Detail behind \S{}6: the stale action across models and horizons, the spliced windows' cost, and the pipeline rows the ledger confirms.

\textbf{The held-out pair's per-trajectory values (\S{}6).} \S{}6's sentence on the held-out pair at h = 368 ended "…, with per-trajectory values +0.8\%, +9.8\%, +14.8\%, +6.5\% and +2.4\%, +5.8\%, +8.3\%, -0.3\%", relative-L1 and nRMSE.

\textbf{Our models under the stale pairing (\S{}6).} For models trained under the causal pairing, the stale one also raises error, and how much depends on the arena. On their own training episodes' 16 trajectories it raises our 10,000-iteration Arm A's relative-L1 error by +124.6\% [+75.8, +201.9] at h = 1 and +209.3\% [+167.3, +257.6] at h = 100; on the held-out pair's 4 it changes it by -1.0\% [-6.1, +27.7] at h = 1, which those trajectories cannot resolve, and by +16.6\% [+1.9, +72.6] at h = 100 (three-seed means, computed post hoc, \texttt{results/\allowbreak{}n5\_\allowbreak{}stale\_\allowbreak{}armA\_\allowbreak{}ci\allowbreak{}.json}; Appendix N gives every model at every horizon). Whether the action matters to them at all is rule X2's question, which returns \textbf{RESPONDS TO THE ACTION} below, with its caveat that every action in the data comes from one policy.

\textbf{The forecast shift (\S{}6).} A percentage change depends on how accurate the model already is as much as on how much the action matters: measured as the shift in the forecast itself, the stale action moves our model's forecast at h = 8 by 0.200 and the released checkpoint's by 0.204 on their own training data (Appendix N), so at h = 8 the two respond about equally, and our model's larger percentages there reflect its smaller error, 0.086 against 0.158 relative-L1 (\texttt{results/\allowbreak{}n5\_\allowbreak{}stale\_\allowbreak{}armA\_\allowbreak{}ci\allowbreak{}.json}); at h = 100 the released checkpoint's forecast moves more, 0.373 against 0.246.

Rule X2 was committed and pushed before any of its readings existed (ledger M-84; Appendix E gives its lead time) and is discharged in M-85. It is exploratory and re-opens no rule. It asks whether our trained models condition their forecasts on the action they are given. Only the actions the forecast steps read are changed, so the history and the recurrent state that meets the first forecast action are untouched. E is the change in relative-L1 error, cumulative over forecast steps 1..h, against the true actions: the ratio err(I) / err(true), less one, in percent; for our arms, the mean over three seeds of each seed's E. Its 95\% interval resamples whole trajectories, one resample for all seeds and both interventions in each draw: exact over all 256 ordered resamples on the held-out pair, 20,000 Monte Carlo resamples (seed 0) otherwise. The interventions are the stale action (the released evaluation's offset), another trajectory's actions (a swap, each trajectory taking the next one's), each action dimension's mean over the model's training rows, and the true actions plus Gaussian noise at k times each dimension's training standard deviation (eight draws). \textbf{The readings} are Arm A's, under the swap at h = 8 on its in-sample arena: \textbf{RESPONDS TO THE ACTION} at 2,500 iterations (+227.8\% [+131.6, +398.2]) and at 10,000 (+676.3\% [+454.8, +1060.0]). The criterion was fixed in the rule before any reading existed (M-84), and the first outcome that matches applies: RESPONDS TO THE ACTION if the point estimate is at least +10\% and the interval's lower bound is above zero; ERROR FALLS WITH WRONG ACTIONS if the interval's upper bound is below zero; DOES NOT RESPOND MEASURABLY if its upper bound is below +10\%; otherwise UNRESOLVED. The bar is deliberately low: the one-step-stale action alone raises the released checkpoint's error at h = 8 by 54.4\% over all ten episodes. What the rule establishes is that our forecasts depend on the action. It does not establish that they depend on it correctly: every action in the data comes from one policy, so a forecast under an action that policy would not take has no recorded outcome to check it against. Arm B and the released checkpoint get the same statistic, alongside and not as readings.

\textbf{The design.} We trained a contaminated arm on 7,882 windows, the clean 7,687 plus 195 splices, and, because that confounds \emph{content} with \emph{count}, a duplication control adding the same 195 windows as exact copies of windows already present.

The arm's contamination rate is 2.47\%, against the reference pipeline's 3.53\%. It is deliberately lower: we splice only the 5 boundaries whose \emph{both} sides are training episodes, because 4 of the 9 between the ten full episodes put held-out rows into training, a leakage problem rather than a physics one that would have invalidated our own comparison. So this experiment measures the cost of training on physically impossible transitions, not the reference's full exposure.

Training loss over the final 250 iterations: duplication costs 0.90\%, splicing costs 21.57\%. The bootstrap interval on duplicated $-$ clean is [-0.0061, 0.0215], including zero, so the rise is caused by splice content, not by dataset size.

In rollout, across 32 cells (the held-out pair and the training episodes, 200 and 400-step trajectories, the 500 and 2,500-iteration checkpoints, n\_independent 4 to 39, at two horizons on two metrics), contamination hurts in \textbf{0} of 32 and helps in 9 (Figure 8a). The control is inert, differing from clean in 2 cells.

\textbf{So "costs nothing" is too strong.} Splicing raises training loss by 21.57\% against duplication's 0.90\%, and improves rollout in 9 of 32 cells: measured effects in opposite directions, the signature of regularisation. The spliced windows contain transitions the model cannot fit, it fits the rest less tightly as a result, and it rolls out slightly better. The defensible statement is that \textbf{at this rate the splices do not harm rollout and appear to help slightly. The unmarked boundaries remain a real defect on leakage grounds; the physically-impossible-transition component costs nothing detectable at this rate.}
